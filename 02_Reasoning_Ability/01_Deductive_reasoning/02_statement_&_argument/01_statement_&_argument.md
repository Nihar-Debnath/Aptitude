Absolutely. **Statement & Argument** is one of the easiest topics in deductive reasoning once you understand the logic behind it. Companies like **TCS, Cognizant, Capgemini, Accenture, Wipro, Infosys** ask these questions regularly.

---

# What is a Statement?

A **statement** is simply a sentence that presents a situation, problem, fact, or issue.

Example:

> The government should ban plastic bags.

This is the **statement**.

---

# What is an Argument?

An **argument** is a **reason** given either:

* **For** the statement (supports it), or
* **Against** the statement (opposes it).

An argument answers the question:

> **"Why?"**

---

## Example 1

### Statement

> Mobile phones should be banned in schools.

### Argument 1

> They distract students during class.

✅ Strong Argument

Why?

Because it gives a logical reason directly related to the statement.

---

### Argument 2

> Mobile phones are available in many colors.

❌ Weak Argument

Why?

The color of phones has nothing to do with banning them in schools.

---

# Your Job in the Exam

You **do not decide whether the statement is right or wrong.**

You only judge:

> **Is the argument strong or weak?**

---

# What is a Strong Argument?

A strong argument is:

* Relevant to the statement.
* Logical.
* Practical.
* Based on facts, common sense, or realistic reasoning.
* Helps in making a decision.

---

### Example

Statement:

> Smoking should be banned in public places.

Argument:

> Passive smoking harms other people.

✅ Strong

Because it gives a valid reason.

---

# What is a Weak Argument?

A weak argument is:

* Irrelevant.
* Emotional without logic.
* Personal opinion.
* Based on assumptions.
* Exaggerated.
* Doesn't help decide the issue.

---

Example

Statement:

> Smoking should be banned in public places.

Argument:

> Smokers look cool.

❌ Weak

It doesn't logically support or oppose the statement.

---

# Strong vs Weak

## Statement

> Online education should be encouraged.

### Argument

> It allows students from remote areas to access education.

✅ Strong

---

### Argument

> Computers are available in black and white colors.

❌ Weak

Not related.

---

# Golden Rules

Whenever you read an argument, ask yourself these five questions.

## 1. Is it Relevant?

Does it talk about the statement?

Statement:

> Plastic should be banned.

Argument:

> Plastic pollutes rivers.

✅ Relevant

---

Argument:

> Plastic bags come in different colors.

❌ Not relevant.

---

## 2. Is it Logical?

Statement

> Helmet should be compulsory.

Argument

> Helmets reduce head injuries.

✅ Logical.

---

Argument

> Helmets make people look funny.

❌ Weak.

---

## 3. Is it Practical?

Statement

> Public transport should be improved.

Argument

> Better transport reduces traffic congestion.

✅ Practical.

---

Argument

> Every person should buy a helicopter.

❌ Not practical.

---

## 4. Is it Based on Facts/Common Sense?

Statement

> Seat belts should be compulsory.

Argument

> Seat belts reduce serious injuries during accidents.

✅ Strong.

---

Argument

> Seat belts attract bad luck.

❌ Weak.

---

## 5. Is it Emotional?

Statement

> Exams should be removed.

Argument

> Students cry during exams.

❌ Weak.

Being emotional isn't enough.

---

Argument

> Exams help measure students' understanding.

✅ Strong.

---

# Types of Weak Arguments

## 1. Personal Opinion

Statement

> Homework should be compulsory.

Argument

> I don't like homework.

❌ Weak

---

## 2. Irrelevant

Statement

> Plastic should be banned.

Argument

> Plastic factories employ many workers.

This may be relevant **against** the ban if it explains economic impact. Simply mentioning employment isn't enough—you'd need to connect it to the decision.

A stronger version would be:

> Banning plastic immediately could cause large job losses unless alternatives are provided.

✅ Strong (opposes the statement logically)

---

## 3. Emotional

Statement

> Zoo should be closed.

Argument

> Animals are cute.

❌ Weak.

---

## 4. Extreme

Statement

> Internet should be free.

Argument

> Every website should become free forever.

❌ Weak.

Absolute claims without reasoning are often weak.

# Strong Words vs Weak Words

Unlike **Statement & Conclusion**, this topic is **not mainly about words like "All" or "Some."**

Instead, it's about the **quality of the reasoning**.

You should ask:

> "If I were a decision-maker, would this argument actually help me decide?"

If **yes** → Strong.

If **no** → Weak.

---

# Common Mistakes

### ❌ Mistake 1

Thinking emotionally.

Example

> Poor people need help.

Don't judge with your emotions.

Judge whether the argument provides a logical reason.

---

### ❌ Mistake 2

Using your personal opinion.

The exam doesn't ask:

> "Do you agree?"

It asks:

> "Is the argument logically strong?"

---

### ❌ Mistake 3

Using outside knowledge.

Only use:

* Common sense
* The information in the statement
* Basic facts

Don't invent extra facts.

---

# Shortcut for Placements

Whenever you see an argument, ask these questions in order:

1. **Is it related to the statement?**

   * No → ❌ Weak
   * Yes → Continue

2. **Does it give a logical reason?**

   * No → ❌ Weak
   * Yes → Continue

3. **Would this reason help a policymaker or decision-maker make a decision?**

   * Yes → ✅ Strong
   * No → ❌ Weak

---

# Statement & Conclusion vs Statement & Argument

| Statement & Conclusion                                              | Statement & Argument                                                           |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Checks whether the conclusion logically follows from the statement. | Checks whether the reason is strong enough to support or oppose the statement. |
| Focus on inference.                                                 | Focus on reasoning.                                                            |
| Watch for strong vs weak quantifiers (All, Some, May, etc.).        | Watch for relevant, logical, practical reasons.                                |
| Ask: "Does this necessarily follow?"                                | Ask: "Is this a good reason?"                                                  |

---

## Memory Trick

* **Statement & Conclusion** → **"Does it follow?"**
* **Statement & Argument** → **"Is it a good reason?"**

That's the core difference. Once you remember this distinction, you'll find it much easier to identify what each question is testing.
