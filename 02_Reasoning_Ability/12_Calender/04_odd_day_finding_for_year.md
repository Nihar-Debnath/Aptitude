Yes bro — **100%**. 🔥 In calendar reasoning, **remainders of 4 and 7 are two very important shortcuts**, but they are used for **different purposes**.

## 1. Remainder when dividing by 4 → finding leap years

Why 4?

Because normally:

\[
\boxed{\text{Every 4th year is a leap year}}
\]

So when dealing with a number of years, dividing by 4 tells you roughly **how many leap years are present**.

Example:

### 47 years

\[
47\div4=11\text{ remainder }3
\]

So there are **11 complete groups of 4**, meaning roughly **11 leap years**.

Then:

* Leap years = 11
* Ordinary years = (47-11=36)

Odd days:

\[
36(1)+11(2)=58
\]

\[
58\div7=8\text{ remainder }2
\]

So 47 years → **2 odd days**.

---

# 2. Remainder when dividing by 7 → finding odd days

Why 7?

Because:

\[
\boxed{1\text{ week}=7\text{ days}}
\]

So when you divide total days by 7, the **remainder is the odd days**.

Example:

\[
365\div7
\]

\[
365=7(52)+1
\]

Remainder:

\[
\boxed{1}
\]

Therefore:

**1 ordinary year = 1 odd day.**

For a leap year:

\[
366\div7
\]

Remainder:

\[
\boxed{2}
\]

Therefore:

**1 leap year = 2 odd days.**

---

# 🔥 So remember this distinction

| Divide by | Why?            | Used for                      |
| --------- | --------------- | ----------------------------- |
| **4**     | Leap-year cycle | Find number of leap years     |
| **7**     | Week = 7 days   | Find odd days / weekday shift |

### Think:

\[
\boxed{\div4\rightarrow\text{LEAP}}
\]

\[
\boxed{\div7\rightarrow\text{ODD DAYS}}
\]

---

### One important warning

Don't blindly say:

> "Remainder of 4 = number of leap years."

It's a **shortcut**, and century years need the **100/400 rule**.

For example:

\[
100\div4=25
\]

But there are only **24 leap years** in years 1–100 because **100 is not a leap year**.

So the proper order is:

**First determine leap years → then calculate odd days → finally take remainder ÷7.**

That's the core machinery behind almost the entire calendar chapter.
