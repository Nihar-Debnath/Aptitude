Yes! **This one is different from the previous example precisely because the date is January.**

Given:

\[
15\text{ Jan }1848=\text{Monday}
\]

Find:

\[
15\text{ Jan }1862=?
\]

### 1. Year difference

\[
1862-1848=14
\]

So there are **14 years**.

### 2. Find leap years crossed

Now the starting date is **15 January 1848**.

The leap day of 1848:

\[
29\text{ February 1848}
\]

is still **ahead of us**.

So **1848 counts**. ✅

Other leap years crossed:

* 1848 ✅
* 1852 ✅
* 1856 ✅
* 1860 ✅

So:

\[
\boxed{4\text{ leap years}}
\]

### 3. Calculate odd days

14 ordinary-year contributions:

\[
14
\]

Extra odd day for each of the 4 leap years:

\[
4
\]

Total:

\[
14+4=18
\]

Reduce by 7:

\[
18-14=\boxed{4}
\]

So the date moves **4 days forward**.

### 4. Move from Monday

\[
Monday\rightarrow Tuesday\rightarrow Wednesday\rightarrow Thursday\rightarrow Friday
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\text{15 January 1862 = Friday}}
\]

### 🔥 Compare the two examples

**15 March 1848 → 15 March 1862**

1848 ❌ because Feb 29 had already passed
→ **3 leap years**

**15 January 1848 → 15 January 1862**

1848 ✅ because Feb 29 is still ahead
→ **4 leap years**

That's the key rule:

> **If Feb 29 of the starting year is still ahead of your starting date → count that leap year.**
> **If Feb 29 has already passed → don't count it.**
