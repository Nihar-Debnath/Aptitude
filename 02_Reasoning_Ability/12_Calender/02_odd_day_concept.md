# 1. Basic structure of a year

A year has:

\[
\boxed{12\text{ months}}
\]

There are two types of years:

### Ordinary year

\[
\boxed{365\text{ days}}
\]

### Leap year

\[
\boxed{366\text{ days}}
\]

Why does a leap year have one extra day?

Because February gets **29 days instead of 28**.

So:

* Ordinary February = 28 days
* Leap February = 29 days

---

# 2. How many weeks are in a year?

One week:

\[
\boxed{7\text{ days}}
\]

Now divide 365 by 7:

\[
365\div7
\]

\[
365=7\times52+1
\]

So an ordinary year contains:

\[
\boxed{52\text{ complete weeks}+1\text{ day}}
\]

That's why people say an ordinary year has **52 weeks + 1 day**.

---

### Leap year

\[
366=7\times52+2
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{52\text{ complete weeks}+2\text{ days}}
\]

So don't think a year has exactly 52 weeks.

It's:

**Ordinary:**

\[
52\text{ weeks}+1\text{ day}
\]

**Leap:**

\[
52\text{ weeks}+2\text{ days}
\]

---

# 3. How do I find a Leap Year?

This is extremely important.

### Basic rule:

A year divisible by **4** is a leap year.

Examples:

\[
2024\div4=506
\]

So 2024 = ✅ leap year.

\[
2028\div4=507
\]

So 2028 = ✅ leap year.

But:

\[
2025\div4
\]

doesn't divide exactly.

So 2025 = ❌ ordinary year.

---

## ⚠️ The century exception

Years ending in **00** are special.

For example:

* 1700
* 1800
* 1900
* 2000
* 2100

Don't just check divisibility by 4.

Check whether they're divisible by **400**.

### 1900

\[
1900\div400
\]

Not exact.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{1900=\text{ordinary}}
\]

### 2000

\[
2000\div400=5
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{2000=\text{leap}}
\]

---

## 🔥 Effective trick

Remember:

> **÷4 = Leap**
> **But if it ends in 00 → check ÷400**

That's enough for almost every exam question.

---

# 4. How many leap years are there in 100 years?

This is slightly tricky.

You might immediately think:

\[
100\div4=25
\]

So 25.

But there's a catch.

Every 100 years contains one **century year**.

For example:

**1–100**

The multiples of 4 are:

\[
4,8,12,\ldots,100
\]

There are:

\[
25
\]

But **100 is NOT a leap year**, because 100 isn't divisible by 400.

Therefore:

\[
25-1=\boxed{24}
\]

So generally:

\[
\boxed{24\text{ leap years in 100 years}}
\]

---

## But there is one important exception

Look at:

**1901–2000**

There are 25 multiples of 4.

And:

\[
2000\div400=5
\]

So **2000 IS a leap year**.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{25\text{ leap years}}
\]

### So don't blindly memorize "100 years = 24."

For calendar questions:

* Most 100-year blocks → **24 leap years**
* A 100-year block containing a year divisible by 400 at its end → **25**

For example:

| Period    | Leap years |
| --------- | ---------: |
| 1–100     |         24 |
| 101–200   |         24 |
| 110–1200   |         **25** |
| 1801–1900 |         24 |
| 1901–2000 |     **25** |
| 2001–2100 |         24 |

---

# 5. What is an Ordinary Year?

An ordinary year simply means:

> **A year that is NOT a leap year.**

It has:

\[
\boxed{365\text{ days}}
\]

Examples:

2023 → ordinary
2025 → ordinary
2026 → ordinary

---

# 6. Why does an ordinary year = 1 Odd Day?

Now we come to the most important concept.

First understand:

\[
\boxed{1\text{ week}=7\text{ days}}
\]

Suppose I give you **14 days**.

That's:

\[
7+7
\]

Two complete weeks.

Nothing is left.

So:

\[
14\div7=2\text{ remainder }0
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{14\text{ days}=0\text{ odd days}}
\]

---

Now take **15 days**:

\[
15=14+1
\]

There are 2 complete weeks + 1 leftover day.

That leftover is the **odd day**.

So:

\[
\boxed{15\text{ days}=1\text{ odd day}}
\]

---

### Therefore:

> **Odd days = the days left after removing complete groups of 7.**

Mathematically:

\[
\boxed{\text{Odd days}=\text{remainder when days are divided by 7}}
\]

---

# 7. Why does 365 days give 1 Odd Day?

Now apply that idea to an ordinary year.

\[
365\div7
\]

We know:

\[
7\times52=364
\]

So:

\[
365=364+1
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{365=52\text{ weeks}+1\text{ day}}
\]

That leftover 1 day is the odd day.

Hence:

\[
\boxed{1\text{ ordinary year}=1\text{ odd day}}
\]

---

# 8. Why does a Leap Year give 2 Odd Days?

Leap year:

\[
366\text{ days}
\]

And:

\[
7\times52=364
\]

Therefore:

\[
366=364+2
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{366=52\text{ weeks}+2\text{ days}}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{1\text{ leap year}=2\text{ odd days}}
\]

---

# 9. Why are Odd Days useful?

THIS is why the whole concept matters.

Suppose:

**Today = Monday**

What day will it be after **365 days**?

We don't need to count 365 days.

Because:

\[
365=52\text{ weeks}+1
\]

52 complete weeks bring us back to Monday.

Only the extra **1 day** matters.

So:

Monday + 1 day = **Tuesday**

Therefore:

> After one ordinary year, the same date shifts forward by **1 weekday**.

---

For a leap year:

\[
366=52\text{ weeks}+2
\]

So:

Monday + 2 = **Wednesday**

That's why:

* Ordinary year → weekday shifts **1**
* Leap year → weekday shifts **2**

---

Everything you've learned connects like this:

```text
                 CALENDAR
                    │
             1 year = 365/366
                    │
             divide by 7
                    │
              complete weeks
                    +
               leftover
                    │
                 ODD DAYS
                    │
       ┌────────────┴────────────┐
       │                         │
 Ordinary year                Leap year
  365 days                    366 days
       │                         │
  52 weeks + 1              52 weeks + 2
       │                         │
  1 odd day                  2 odd days
```

And for months:

```text
31 days → 3 odd days
30 days → 2 odd days
28 days → 0 odd days
29 days → 1 odd day
```
