Pattern Recognition is one of the **most important reasoning skills** in placement exams like **TCS, Cognizant, Capgemini, Infosys, Accenture, Wipro, HCL**, and many others.

It simply means:

> **Finding the hidden rule or relationship and using it to predict or solve something.**

Think of yourself as a detective. You don't memorize answers—you discover the pattern.

---

# Real-Life Example

Imagine this sequence of traffic lights:

🟢 🟡 🔴 🟢 🟡 🔴 🟢 🟡 ?

What comes next?

You notice the repeating pattern:

Green → Yellow → Red

So the answer is:

🔴

That is pattern recognition.

Almost every reasoning question is based on recognizing a pattern.

For example,


+2 every time

Answer:

10

```

```

Here, the pattern isn't the numbers themselves but the changing differences.

# How to Identify a Pattern (Step-by-Step)

Whenever you face a reasoning question, check in this order:

1. **Look for a constant change.**

   * Is it always `+2`, `−5`, or `×3`?

2. **Check the differences.**

   * Example: `5, 9, 15, 23`
   * Differences: `4, 6, 8` → increasing by 2.

3. **Check alternate terms.**

   * Example: `2, 5, 4, 10, 6, 15`
   * Odd positions: `2, 4, 6`
   * Even positions: `5, 10, 15`

4. **Look for squares, cubes, or prime numbers.**

5. **Check alphabet positions.**

   * Convert letters to numbers if needed.

6. **See if the sequence repeats.**

   * Example: `A B C A B C ...`

7. **Think logically.**

   * In coding, family relations, seating arrangements, and puzzles, the "pattern" is often a relationship rather than a numerical rule.

## Why is pattern recognition important?

Pattern recognition is the **foundation of reasoning ability**. Almost every reasoning topic depends on it in some way. Once you get good at spotting patterns quickly, you'll solve questions on number series, letter series, coding-decoding, analogies, odd one out, puzzles, seating arrangements, and many other topics much faster.

For placement exams like **TCS, Cognizant, Infosys, Wipro, Capgemini, and Accenture**, developing this skill is often more valuable than memorizing hundreds of question types, because many questions are simply different ways of testing your ability to identify the underlying rule.
