This is actually a very good question because it teaches **two grammar concepts at once**:

1. **Articles (a, an, the)**
2. **Countable vs. Uncountable nouns**

Let's solve it step by step.

---

# Question

> **I often suffer from ______ backache.**

Options:

A. the

B. a

C. an

D. No article

---

# Step 1: Find the noun

The noun is

> **backache**

Now ask yourself:

> Is **backache** countable or uncountable?

---

## What is a countable noun?

A noun you can count.

Examples

* one book
* two books
* three books

These can take **a/an**.

```
a book
a pen
an apple
```

---

## What is an uncountable noun?

A noun you cannot normally count.

Examples

* water
* milk
* rice
* sugar
* furniture
* advice
* information

You **cannot** say

❌ a water

❌ an information

❌ a furniture

---

# Step 2: What about "backache"?

This is where students get confused.

The word **backache** has **two uses**.

### Use 1 (General pain) → Uncountable ✅

> I suffer from backache.

Meaning:

I have back pain in general.

Here **backache** behaves like an uncountable noun.

So we use **no article**.

---

### Use 2 (One particular pain) → Countable

Example

> I have **a backache** today.

Meaning

One episode of back pain.

Here it is countable.

---

# Step 3: Which meaning is used here?

The sentence says

> **I often suffer from ______ backache.**

Notice the words

> **often suffer from**

This talks about a **general medical condition**, not one specific attack.

So the meaning is

> I often suffer from back pain.

General condition → uncountable

Therefore

✅ No article.

---

# Why not "a"?

If we write

> I often suffer from **a** backache.

It sounds like

> I repeatedly suffer from one particular backache.

English doesn't usually say this.

For general illnesses after **suffer from**, we normally don't use an article.

Examples

✅ suffer from cancer

✅ suffer from diabetes

✅ suffer from asthma

✅ suffer from backache

Notice:

No article.

---

# Why not "the"?

"The" means a **specific** backache.

Example

> The backache I had yesterday is gone.

Now we're talking about one particular backache.

But the question is talking generally.

So

❌ the

---

# Why not "an"?

"Backache" starts with the sound **B**.

B is a consonant sound.

So even if an article were needed, **an** would never be correct.

---

# Compare these sentences

### General meaning

✅ I suffer from backache.

(No article)

---

### One attack

✅ I have **a backache** today.

(A = one episode)

---

### Particular pain

✅ The backache I had last week was terrible.

(The = specific backache)

---

# Another example

### Fever

General statement

> He has fever.

(In modern standard English, this is usually expressed as **He has a fever**, because *fever* is typically treated as countable in this context.)

Specific fever

> The fever has gone down.

---

### Headache

> I have **a headache**.

This is countable because it refers to one headache.

But compare:

> I suffer from **headaches**.

Here we often use the plural to talk about a recurring problem.

---

# A shortcut for placement exams

Whenever you see:

> suffer from ______

Think:

Is it talking about a **general disease/condition**?

Examples:

* suffer from cancer ✅
* suffer from diabetes ✅
* suffer from malaria ✅
* suffer from backache ✅

Usually **no article** is needed when the noun is being used as a general condition.

---

## Final Answer

✅ **D. No article**

Because **backache** is being used to mean **back pain in general**, and after **suffer from** we commonly use **no article** for a general medical condition.

---

### One important note

English has a few health-related words that can behave differently depending on the meaning. For example:

* ✅ I suffer from **backache**. (general condition)
* ✅ I have **a backache**. (one episode)

This is why understanding the **meaning of the sentence** is just as important as knowing the article rules.
