This is a **Sentence Improvement** question.

### Original sentence

> **It is probable that the prototype cellular motor might be ready for testing around the end of next year.**

The underlined part is:

> **might be ready for testing around the end of next year**

### Step 1: Find the error

The phrase **"It is probable that"** already expresses **possibility/probability**.

Adding **"might"** again makes the sentence **redundant**.

* ❌ It is probable that ... **might** ...
* ✅ It is probable that ... **will** ...

Compare:

* It is likely that he **will** come. ✅
* It is likely that he **might** come. ❌ (double uncertainty)

### Step 2: Check the options

**A.** might be ready for testing around the end of next year
❌ Keeps the redundancy.

**B.** may be ready for testing about he end of next year
❌ Still redundant ("It is probable" + "may"), and it also has a typo ("he end").

**C.** might be ready for testing toward next year's end
❌ Still uses **might**.

**D.** will be ready for testing toward the end of next year
✅ Correct. Removes the redundancy and is grammatically natural.

**E.** should be ready for testing toward the end of next year
❌ "Should" expresses expectation, not probability in the same sense. It's less appropriate than **will** after "It is probable that."

## ✅ Correct Answer: **D**

### Quick exam trick

Whenever you see:

* **It is likely that...**
* **It is probable that...**
* **There is a good chance that...**

avoid adding another modal of possibility (**may, might, could**) unless the sentence specifically requires it.

Examples:

* ✅ It is likely that it **will** rain.
* ❌ It is likely that it **might** rain.

# Step 1: Read the sentence completely

> **It is probable that the prototype cellular motor might be ready for testing around the end of next year.**

Before looking at the options, ask yourself:

> **What is this sentence trying to say?**

It means:

> There is a good chance that the prototype will be ready near the end of next year.

---

# Step 2: Find the important words

There are two important parts.

### Part 1

> **It is probable that**

What does **probable** mean?

* probable = likely
* probable = expected
* probable = there is a good chance

So this part **already tells us the event is uncertain but likely.**

Think of it as:

```
probable = maybe (80% chance)
```

---

### Part 2

Then comes

> **might be ready**

Now ask yourself:

What does **might** mean?

It also means

* maybe
* perhaps
* possibly

So now the sentence becomes

> It is probable that...
>
> ...might...

Both express possibility.

So the sentence is saying

> Maybe... maybe...

which is unnecessary.

That's why it sounds awkward.

---

## Think of another example

### Wrong

> It is likely that he **might** come.

Both "likely" and "might" show uncertainty.

---

### Better

> It is likely that he **will** come.

Here,

* "likely" gives the uncertainty.
* "will come" tells what is expected.

Only one word expresses probability.

## Option E

> should be ready for testing toward the end of next year

Many students think this is correct.

But notice the meaning.

### "should"

does **not** mean probable.

It means

* expected
* ought to
* according to the plan

Example

> The train should arrive at 5.

This means

"We expect it to."

It doesn't exactly match

> It is probable...

which simply talks about likelihood.

So **will** fits better.

❌ Not the best answer.

# A shortcut for placement exams

Whenever you see words like

* probably
* likely
* probable
* certainly
* definitely
* surely

they already express certainty or probability.

Then check whether another modal verb is repeating the same idea.

| Word already in sentence | Avoid   |
| ------------------------ | ------- |
| probable                 | may     |
| probable                 | might   |
| likely                   | could   |
| certainly                | maybe   |
| definitely               | perhaps |

# Exam Approach (10-second method)

Whenever you get a Sentence Improvement question:

1. **Read the whole sentence** to understand the meaning.
2. **Look for grammar mistakes** (tense, subject-verb agreement, articles, prepositions, modals, redundancy).
3. **Compare each option** with the original sentence.
4. **Eliminate options** that keep the same mistake.
5. Choose the option that is **grammatically correct** and **preserves the original meaning**.
