# Question

> **(A)** Sprouting home cleaning can be a tiny
>
> **(B)** and imperative step in
>
> **(C)** make our surrounding much better.

Options:

1. Error in A
2. Error in B
3. Error in C
4. Error in both B and C
5. No Error

---

# Step 1: Read the whole sentence

> Sprouting home cleaning can be a tiny and imperative step in make our surrounding much better.

Immediately, one phrase sounds wrong:

> **step in make**

Let's investigate.

---

# Step 2: Check Part A

> Sprouting home cleaning can be a tiny

This sounds strange, but let's check grammar.

The phrase **sprouting home cleaning** is not common English. It probably intends something like:

* Starting home cleaning
* Promoting home cleaning

However, **sprouting** is a present participle and can technically modify a noun in some contexts.

So although it's awkward, it isn't the clearest grammar error being tested.

---

# Step 3: Check Part B

> and imperative step in

This is correct.

* tiny and imperative → two adjectives describing "step"

No grammar problem here.

---

# Step 4: Check Part C

> **make** our surrounding much better

Now ask:

The sentence says:

> step **in** make

Do we say

> in make ❌

or

> in making ✅

---

## Rule

After a preposition (**in, on, at, for, by, of, about, after, before, without**, etc.), we use:

> **Verb + ing (Gerund)**

Examples:

✅ interested **in learning**

✅ good **at playing**

✅ before **leaving**

✅ after **eating**

✅ by **working**

❌ interested in learn

❌ before leave

❌ after eat

---

Here:

> step **in make** ❌

should be

> step **in making** ✅

---

# Correct Sentence

> Sprouting home cleaning can be a tiny and imperative step in **making** our surroundings much better.

---

# There is another small issue

The sentence says:

> our **surrounding**

Usually we say:

> our **surroundings**

because **surroundings** means "the environment around us."

Examples:

* Keep your surroundings clean.
* Beautiful surroundings improve our mood.

So the most natural sentence is:

> ...making our **surroundings** much better.

---

# So why is the answer only "C"?

Because Part C contains both:

* ❌ make → making
* ❌ surrounding → surroundings

Both mistakes are inside **Part C**.

---

# Grammar Rule Learned Today

## Rule: Preposition + Verb

Whenever you see a preposition, check the next verb.

| Preposition | Correct              |
| ----------- | -------------------- |
| in          | in **reading**       |
| on          | on **working**       |
| at          | at **playing**       |
| for         | for **helping**      |
| by          | by **studying**      |
| after       | after **eating**     |
| before      | before **leaving**   |
| without     | without **speaking** |

Never write:

❌ in read

❌ after eat

❌ before go

---

# Final Answer

✅ **Option 3 — Error in C**

Corrections:

* **make** → **making**
* **surrounding** → **surroundings**

---

## A trick for error spotting

Whenever you see a sentence with words like:

* in
* on
* at
* by
* after
* before
* without
* for
* of

**Immediately look at the next verb.**

If it's in the **base form** (`go`, `make`, `eat`, `learn`), there's a good chance it should be a **gerund** (`going`, `making`, `eating`, `learning`).

This is one of the most frequently tested grammar patterns in placement exams like TCS NQT, Infosys, Cognizant, and Accenture.

## Original sentence

> **(A)** Sprouting home cleaning can be a tiny
>
> **(B)** and imperative step in
>
> **(C)** make our surrounding much better.

Let's join it:

> **Sprouting home cleaning can be a tiny and imperative step in make our surrounding much better.**

---

# Error in C (Definitely)

This one is clear.

> step **in make** ❌

After a preposition (**in**), we use a **gerund (Verb + ing)**.

So:

> step in **making** ✅

Also,

> our **surrounding** ❌

should be

> our **surroundings** ✅

So C definitely has an error.

---

# Why does the answer key say B also?

Look carefully at Part B:

> **and imperative step in**

The word **imperative** means:

> extremely important, essential

The word **tiny** means:

> very small

So the sentence says:

> **a tiny and imperative step**

This is **not a grammar error**.

It's perfectly grammatical.

For example:

* A small but important step.
* A tiny yet essential step.

Those are grammatically fine.

---

## Then why did they mark B?

Because they probably expected:

> **a tiny but imperative step**

instead of

> **a tiny and imperative step**

Compare:

### Using **and**

> A tiny and important step.

This means the step is:

* tiny ✅
* important ✅

Grammatically correct.

---

### Using **but**

> A tiny but important step.

This emphasizes a contrast:

> Although it is small, it is important.

This sounds **more natural**.

---

# Is "and" wrong?

**No.**

It is **less expressive**, but not grammatically incorrect.

Native speakers say things like:

* A small and effective solution.
* A tiny and useful tool.
* A short and meaningful speech.

These are all correct.

---

# So what's the real situation?

There are two possibilities.

### Possibility 1 (Most likely)

The question setter wanted to test **meaning**, not just grammar.

They expected:

> tiny **but** imperative

because "tiny" contrasts with "important."

---

### Possibility 2

The answer key is simply **wrong**.

This happens quite often in placement-preparation PDFs and coaching materials.

---

# My advice for exams

There are two types of questions.

## Grammar-based

Example:

> Everyone were...

Clearly wrong.

---

## Meaning-based

Example:

> tiny and imperative

The grammar is fine.

The meaning is slightly awkward.

Some exam setters count this.

Some don't.
