This question tests a very common grammar rule:

> **Modal Verbs (shall, will, can, may, must, should, etc.)**

Let's solve it step by step.

---

# Step 1: Read the sentence

> **The State, in turn, shall (1) / immediately appointed one of the persons (2) / shortlisted by the commission. (3)**

Forget the divisions.

Read it as one sentence:

> The State, in turn, shall immediately appointed one of the persons shortlisted by the commission.

---

# Step 2: Find the helping verb

The helping verb is

> **shall**

Now recall this rule.

---

# Rule: After a Modal Verb, always use the Base Form (V1)

Modal verbs include:

* shall
* will
* can
* could
* may
* might
* must
* should
* would

After these, use **V1 (base form)**.

### Examples

✅ She **will go**.

❌ She **will went**.

---

✅ You **can swim**.

❌ You **can swam**.

---

✅ He **should study**.

❌ He **should studied**.

---

# Step 3: Apply the rule

Sentence says:

> shall **appointed**

"Appointed" is **V2/V3**, not the base form.

After **shall**, we need **appoint**.

Correct sentence:

> **The State, in turn, shall immediately appoint one of the persons shortlisted by the commission.**

---

# Step 4: Find the error

### Part (1)

> shall

Correct.

---

### Part (2)

> immediately appointed one of the persons

Error is here.

It should be

> immediately **appoint**

---

### Part (3)

> shortlisted by the commission

This is correct.

"Shortlisted" is a **past participle** describing **persons**.

It means:

> the persons **who were shortlisted** by the commission.

No problem.

# Why is "shortlisted" correct but "appointed" wrong?

This confuses many students.

Look at the sentence structure.

### First verb (Main action)

> The State shall appoint...

Here "appoint" is the **main verb**.

Since it comes after **shall**, it must be **V1**.

---

### Second verb (Describing the noun)

> persons shortlisted by the commission

Here "shortlisted" is **not the main verb**.

It is an adjective-like **past participle** describing **persons**.

Think of it as:

> persons **who were shortlisted** by the commission.

So "shortlisted" stays in V3 form.

---

## Visualize the sentence

```text
The State
      │
      ├── shall appoint   ← Main verb (V1 after shall)
      │
      └── one of the persons
                  │
                  └── shortlisted by the commission
                      (describes "persons")
```

# A Similar Example

✅ The company **will hire** employees **trained** in AI.

Notice:

* **will hire** → main verb → V1 after modal.
* **trained** → describes employees.

Not:

❌ The company will **hired** employees.

Ah! Now I understand what you meant. You were asking about the **special relationship between "shall" and the first/second/third person**, not about modal verbs in general.

This is actually an **old English grammar rule** that competitive exams still like to test.

Let's understand it properly.

---

# The Traditional Rule

## For Simple Future

| Person                                             | Use       |
| -------------------------------------------------- | --------- |
| **I, We** (First Person)                           | **shall** |
| **You, He, She, It, They** (Second & Third Person) | **will**  |

Examples:

✅ I shall go.

✅ We shall win.

✅ You will come.

✅ He will help.

This is the traditional rule taught in grammar books.

---

# Why?

Originally:

* **shall** = simple future with **I/We**
* **will** = simple future with everyone else

So people learned:

```text
I shall
We shall

You will
He will
She will
They will
```

---

# But then comes the REVERSE RULE

Suppose you want to show **strong determination, promise, command, or insistence**.

Then the rule **reverses**.

| Person                     | Use       |
| -------------------------- | --------- |
| **I, We**                  | **will**  |
| **You, He, She, It, They** | **shall** |

This is the part shown in your screenshot.

---

# Why does it reverse?

Because in old English:

* **will** expressed the speaker's determination.
* **shall** expressed obligation or command.

---

## Example 1 (First Person)

### Simple future

> I shall finish the work tomorrow.

Meaning:

> I expect to finish tomorrow.

No special emphasis.

---

### Determination

> I will finish the work tomorrow.

Meaning:

> I am determined to finish it.

There is strong intention.

---

## Example 2 (Third Person)

### Simple future

> He will come tomorrow.

Just a future event.

---

### Command / Obligation

> He shall come tomorrow.

Meaning:

> He **must** come.

This is almost like an order.

---

# Now let's come to your screenshot.

The sentence was:

> **The State shall immediately appoint one of the persons...**

Why did the teacher say **shall** is correct?

Because this is **not talking about the future**.

The State is making a **legal obligation**.

It means:

> The State **must** appoint...

In laws, constitutions, contracts, and official documents, **shall** is extremely common.

Examples:

> The tenant shall pay the rent on the first day of every month.

Meaning:

> The tenant is required to pay.

Not merely "will pay."

---

Another example:

> The President shall appoint the judges.

Meaning:

The Constitution requires it.

Not merely a future event.

---

# Modern English

Nowadays, native speakers rarely follow the old "shall/will" person rule.

Instead, they usually say:

> I will go.

> We will come.

> He will arrive.

Even with **I** and **We**, **will** is normal.

"Shall" is mainly used in:

* legal documents
* contracts
* rules
* formal writing
* offers and suggestions

Examples:

> Shall we begin?

> Shall I help you?

---

# Memory Table

| Situation                   | First Person       | Second & Third Person |
| --------------------------- | ------------------ | --------------------- |
| Simple Future (Traditional) | shall              | will                  |
| Determination/Promise       | will               | shall                 |
| Modern English              | will               | will                  |
| Legal/Official Language     | shall (obligation) | shall (obligation)    |

---

# For Competitive Exams

Remember these three points:

### 1. If the sentence is about **future** (traditional grammar)

```text
I/We → shall
You/He/She/They → will
```

---

### 2. If the sentence shows **determination, promise, threat, command, or obligation**

The rule reverses:

```text
I/We → will
You/He/She/They → shall
```

---

### 3. If the sentence is from a **law, constitution, contract, or official rule**

**"shall"** is almost always correct because it means:

> **must / is required to**

Example:

> The State shall appoint...

Here, **shall** does **not** mean simple future. It expresses a legal duty or obligation.

---

This is why the teacher emphasized that **many students wrongly think "The State shall..." is incorrect** because "State" is third person. In ordinary future grammar, they would expect **will**, but **legal English uses "shall" to impose an obligation**, so **"The State shall appoint..." is perfectly correct.**
