---

# Step 1: Read the complete sentence

> **A visible furious boy even started making his way towards the tunnel before turning back to take his place on the touchline towards the end of extra-time.**

Don't look at the options first.

Read it once naturally.

---

# Step 2: Find the suspicious words

The phrase

> **visible furious boy**

sounds strange.

Ask yourself:

What does **visible** describe?

It describes **boy**.

But is the boy **visible**, or is he **furious** in a visible way?

The intended meaning is:

> The boy was **visibly furious**.

---

# Step 3: Apply the Adjective vs Adverb Rule

This question tests a very common grammar rule.

## Rule

* **Adjective** → modifies a **noun**.
* **Adverb** → modifies a **verb, adjective, or another adverb**.

---

### Here

The adjective is:

> **furious**

Who is furious?

The **boy**.

So **furious** correctly describes the noun **boy**.

Now look at **visible**.

Is it describing the boy?

No.

It is describing **how furious** the boy was.

It modifies the adjective **furious**.

Whenever a word modifies an adjective, it must be an **adverb**.

So

❌ visible furious

✅ visibly furious

---

## Compare

### Wrong

> She is beautiful smart.

"Beautiful" cannot modify another adjective.

---

### Correct

> She is **beautifully** dressed.

Here "beautifully" modifies the verb "dressed."

---

Another example

❌ Extreme happy

✅ Extremely happy

---

Another

❌ Complete wrong

✅ Completely wrong

---

Another

❌ Perfect normal

✅ Perfectly normal

---

Our sentence follows the same pattern.

```text
visibly → modifies → furious
furious → modifies → boy
```

---

# Step 4: Find the error part

Part (1)

> A visible furious boy...

This contains the error.

It should be

> **A visibly furious boy...**

So

✅ Error = **Part (1)**

---

# Answer

✅ **Option A (1)**

---

# Visual Structure

```text
          boy (noun)
             ▲
             │
        furious (adjective)
             ▲
             │
      visibly (adverb)
```

Notice the chain.

* **Boy** is a noun.
* **Furious** describes the boy.
* **Visibly** describes **how furious** the boy looked.

---

# Exam Trick

Whenever you see **two describing words together**, ask:

Are they both describing the noun?

or

Is one describing the other?

### Example 1

> A **beautiful red** dress.

* beautiful → dress
* red → dress

Both modify the noun.

✅ Correct.

---

### Example 2

> A **complete different** story.

"Complete" modifies "different."

It should be

> **completely different**

---

### Example 3

> A **real beautiful** painting.

"Real" modifies "beautiful."

Formal grammar expects

> **really beautiful**

---

### Example 4

> A **visible angry** man.

Should be

> **visibly angry**

---

## A Quick Tip for Error Spotting

When you see two descriptive words together, check this:

```text
Word 1 + Word 2 + Noun
```

Ask:

* Do **both words** describe the noun? → Both can be adjectives.
* Does **Word 1 describe Word 2**? → Word 1 should be an adverb.

That's exactly what happened here:

> ❌ visible furious boy

> ✅ visibly furious boy

This **Adjective vs Adverb** mistake is another very common pattern in competitive exams.
