Absolutely. Since you're building grammar **from the root for verbal ability**, let's start with **SENTENCE** properly.

Don't worry about memorizing 50 rules at once. First, I want you to build a mental model of what a sentence actually is. Once that is clear, grammar becomes much easier.

# 1. What is a Sentence?

A **sentence is a group of words that expresses a complete thought/meaning.**

For example:

> **Rahul plays cricket.**

This gives us a complete idea.

But:

> **Rahul playing cricket**

Something feels incomplete. We are expecting something like:

> Rahul **is** playing cricket.

So the first basic idea is:

**Sentence = words arranged in such a way that they express a complete thought.**

---

# 2. A sentence is NOT just a group of words

Look at these:

### A.

> I am learning English.

Complete meaning → ✅ Sentence

### B.

> English learning I am.

Words are present, but arrangement is wrong → ❌

### C.

> Because I was tired.

We understand the meaning, but we are waiting:

> Because I was tired, **I went to bed.**

So C is not a complete sentence when standing alone.

This is extremely important:

> **A sentence needs both grammatical structure AND complete meaning.**

---

# 3. The most important parts of a sentence

For basic grammar, you need to understand these:

1. **Subject**
2. **Verb**
3. **Object**
4. **Complement**
5. **Adverbial / Modifier**

Let's understand them one by one.

---

# 4. Subject

The **subject** is generally the person, thing, or idea that the sentence is talking about or that performs the action.

Example:

> **Rahul** plays cricket.

Who plays?

→ Rahul

Therefore:

**Rahul = Subject**

Another:

> **The dog** is barking.

Who is barking?

→ The dog

Subject = **the dog**

Another:

> **My brother** works in Kolkata.

Who works?

→ My brother

Subject = **my brother**

---

# 5. Verb

The **verb** tells us about an action, state, or occurrence.

Example:

> Rahul **plays** cricket.

"plays" = verb.

> She **runs** fast.

"runs" = verb.

> They **are** happy.

"are" = verb.

Notice something important:

A verb doesn't always mean an obvious physical action.

### Action

> He **runs**.

### State

> He **is** happy.

### Possession

> He **has** a car.

All contain verbs.

---

# 6. Subject + Verb

This is one of the most fundamental sentence structures.

> Birds fly.

Subject → Birds
Verb → fly

> Dogs bark.

Subject → Dogs
Verb → bark

> She sleeps.

Subject → She
Verb → sleeps

So:

**S + V**

is a basic sentence structure.

---

# 7. Object

Now look at:

> Rahul plays cricket.

We know:

Who plays?

→ Rahul = Subject

What does Rahul play?

→ cricket = Object

So:

**Rahul** → Subject
**plays** → Verb
**cricket** → Object

Therefore:

> **S + V + O**

---

Another:

> She reads books.

She → Subject
reads → Verb
books → Object

Another:

> I love English.

I → Subject
love → Verb
English → Object

---

# 8. How do I find the Object?

A very useful beginner technique:

After finding the verb, ask:

> **What?** or **Whom?**

Example:

> Rahul kicked the ball.

Verb = kicked.

Kicked **what?**

→ the ball

Therefore:

Object = **the ball**

---

Example:

> She called her mother.

Verb = called.

Called **whom?**

→ her mother

Object = **her mother**

---

But be careful.

Not every sentence has an object.

> Rahul sleeps.

Rahul sleeps **what?** ❌

There is no object.

So:

> Rahul sleeps.

S + V

while:

> Rahul plays cricket.

S + V + O

---

# 9. Complement

This is where things start getting interesting.

Look at:

> Rahul is a student.

Rahul → Subject
is → Verb
a student → ?

"a student" isn't really receiving an action.

Instead, it **describes/identifies Rahul**.

That's a **complement**.

So:

> Rahul is **a student**.

"a student" = Subject Complement.

---

Another:

> She is beautiful.

She = Subject
is = Verb
beautiful = Subject Complement

Why?

Because **beautiful describes "she."**

---

Compare:

> She reads books.

books = Object

> She is beautiful.

beautiful = Complement

This distinction becomes very important later.

---

# 10. Object vs Complement

Look carefully.

### Sentence 1

> Rahul kicked the ball.

Rahul ≠ ball.

The ball receives the action.

Therefore:

**ball = Object**

### Sentence 2

> Rahul is a teacher.

Rahul = teacher.

"teacher" identifies/describes Rahul.

Therefore:

**teacher = Complement**

A simple mental trick:

> **Object receives the action.**
> **Complement describes or identifies the subject/object.**

We'll go much deeper into this later.

---

# 11. Modifier

A modifier gives additional information.

Example:

> Rahul runs **quickly**.

"quickly" tells us **how** Rahul runs.

It's a modifier.

Another:

> Rahul runs **in the morning**.

It tells us **when**.

Another:

> Rahul lives **in Kolkata**.

It tells us **where**.

Another:

> The **tall** boy is running.

"tall" describes the boy.

So modifiers add extra information.

---

# 12. The basic sentence formula

You can think of a sentence like this:

> **Subject + Verb + everything else**

For example:

> Rahul plays cricket every evening.

Rahul → Subject
plays → Verb
cricket → Object
every evening → Modifier

So:

**S + V + O + Modifier**

---

# 13. Word order is VERY important in English

English generally follows a relatively fixed word order.

The most common pattern is:

> **Subject → Verb → Object**

For example:

> I eat rice.

Not:

> I rice eat. ❌

Not:

> Eat I rice. ❌

This is different from some Indian languages where word order can work differently.

That's why you should develop the habit of seeing:

> **WHO + DOES WHAT + TO WHAT/WHOM**

Example:

> Rohan bought a laptop.

WHO?

→ Rohan

DOES WHAT?

→ bought

TO WHAT?

→ a laptop

---

# 14. Every sentence has a finite verb

This is a concept you will eventually need for **error spotting and sentence correction**.

Consider:

> Rahul **plays** cricket.

"plays" is finite.

It changes according to subject/tense:

> Rahul **plays** cricket.

> They **play** cricket.

The verb changes.

Another:

> She **was** happy.

> They **were** happy.

The verb changes.

This is why finite verbs are important.

Don't worry if "finite verb" feels complicated right now. We'll make it very easy when we study verbs.

---

# 15. Sentence vs Phrase

This is one of the MOST important distinctions.

## Phrase

A phrase is a group of words that **does not contain a complete subject-verb structure expressing a complete thought.**

Examples:

> in the room

> a beautiful girl

> after the class

> very quickly

These aren't complete sentences.

---

## Sentence

> Rahul is in the room.

Complete thought → ✅

---

Compare:

> **after the class**

Phrase.

> **After the class ended**

This contains a subject + verb, but it still doesn't express a complete thought by itself.

That's a **clause**, which we'll discuss next.

---

# 16. Sentence vs Clause

A **clause** is a group of words containing a subject and a verb.

Example:

> Rahul plays cricket.

Subject = Rahul
Verb = plays

So it's a clause.

And because it can stand alone, it's an **independent clause**.

Now:

> because Rahul plays cricket

Subject = Rahul
Verb = plays

It's a clause.

But:

> Because Rahul plays cricket...

We are waiting for more information.

So it cannot stand alone.

It's a **dependent/subordinate clause**.

---

# 17. Clause → Sentence relationship

Think of it like this:

**Words**

↓

**Phrase**

↓

**Clause**

↓

**Sentence**

But don't think every sentence has only one clause.

A sentence can contain **one or multiple clauses.**

For example:

> Rahul plays cricket.

One clause.

But:

> Rahul plays cricket **because he enjoys it.**

Two clauses:

1. Rahul plays cricket.
2. he enjoys it.

Together they form one sentence.

---

# 18. The four types of sentences by PURPOSE

This is a very important classification.

There are **4 major types**.

### 1. Assertive / Declarative

Makes a statement.

> Rahul is a student.

> I like English.

> The Earth revolves around the Sun.

It gives information.

---

### 2. Interrogative

Asks a question.

> Are you ready?

> Where do you live?

> Did Rahul come?

Usually ends with:

**?**

---

### 3. Imperative

Gives a command, order, request, advice, instruction, etc.

> Sit down.

> Open the door.

> Please help me.

> Take care of yourself.

The subject is often **you**, but it is understood.

For example:

> Open the door.

Really means:

> **You** open the door.

But we normally don't say "you."

---

### 4. Exclamatory

Expresses strong emotion.

> What a beautiful day!

> How beautiful she is!

> What an idiot!

Usually ends with:

**!**

---

# 19. Another classification: Structure

This is VERY important for verbal ability.

There are four:

### 1. Simple Sentence

Contains **one independent clause**.

> Rahul plays cricket.

> I study English.

> She went home.

---

### 2. Compound Sentence

Two or more independent clauses joined together.

> Rahul plays cricket **and** I play football.

Clause 1:

> Rahul plays cricket.

Clause 2:

> I play football.

Both can stand independently.

Usually joined by coordinating conjunctions like:

**and, but, or, so, yet, for, nor**

---

### 3. Complex Sentence

Contains:

**One independent clause + at least one dependent clause**

Example:

> I stayed home **because I was sick.**

Independent:

> I stayed home.

Dependent:

> because I was sick.

---

### 4. Compound-Complex Sentence

Contains:

**At least two independent clauses + at least one dependent clause.**

Example:

> I stayed home because I was sick, **and** my brother went to school.

Independent clause 1:

> I stayed home.

Dependent clause:

> because I was sick.

Independent clause 2:

> my brother went to school.

This is the most complicated of the four.

---

# 20. Sentence structure in one picture

Remember this:

```text
SENTENCE
│
├── Simple
│
├── Compound
│
├── Complex
│
└── Compound-Complex
```

And separately:

```text
SENTENCE BY PURPOSE
│
├── Declarative → statement
├── Interrogative → question
├── Imperative → command/request
└── Exclamatory → strong emotion
```

These are **two different ways of classifying sentences.**

This is important.

For example:

> Are you coming because Rahul called you?

It is:

**Interrogative** → because it asks a question.

And:

**Complex** → because it has an independent + dependent clause.

A sentence can belong to **one category from each classification.**

---

# 21. Positive and Negative sentences

Another useful classification.

### Affirmative / Positive

> I like English.

### Negative

> I do not like English.

The negative form usually contains words like:

> not, never, no, nobody, nothing, etc.

This becomes extremely important when we study **auxiliary verbs and sentence correction.**

# 22. What makes a sentence grammatically correct?

A sentence generally needs things like:

### 1. Correct subject-verb agreement

❌ He play cricket.

✅ He plays cricket.

### 2. Correct tense

❌ Yesterday I go to school.

✅ Yesterday I went to school.

### 3. Correct word order

❌ He cricket plays.

✅ He plays cricket.

### 4. Correct articles

❌ He is good boy.

✅ He is a good boy.

### 5. Correct prepositions

❌ He is good in English.

Depending on intended meaning:

✅ He is good at English.

### 6. Correct pronoun

❌ Rahul told Rahul's mother that Rahul was tired.

Usually:

✅ Rahul told his mother that he was tired.

These are exactly the kinds of things that later appear in **sentence correction/error spotting.**

# 23. The BIG idea you should understand

Don't think of grammar as:

> "I have to memorize 500 rules."

Instead, think:

**Grammar = how words are organized to create meaning.**

For example:

> He plays cricket.

We have:

**He** → subject
**plays** → verb
**cricket** → object

Now grammar asks:

### What happens if I change the subject?

> He **plays**.

> They **play**.

That's subject-verb agreement.

### What happens if I change time?

> He plays cricket.

> He played cricket.

> He will play cricket.

That's tense.

### What if I make it negative?

> He does not play cricket.

That's negation.

### What if I ask a question?

> Does he play cricket?

That's interrogation.

### What if I add information?

> He plays cricket **every evening**.

That's modification.

So eventually, **all these grammar topics connect back to the sentence.**

# 24. Your mental model of a sentence

Whenever you see a sentence, **don't immediately look for a grammar rule.**

First ask:

### Step 1 — Who/what is the sentence about?

→ **Subject**

### Step 2 — What is happening / what is the state?

→ **Verb**

### Step 3 — Is something receiving the action?

→ **Object**

### Step 4 — Is something describing/identifying the subject or object?

→ **Complement**

### Step 5 — Is there additional information?

→ **Modifier**

For example:

> **The intelligent boy** **solved** **the difficult problem** **very quickly** **yesterday**.

Subject:

> The intelligent boy

Verb:

> solved

Object:

> the difficult problem

Modifier:

> very quickly

Modifier:

> yesterday

Now you can **see the skeleton of the sentence.**

---

# 25. The sentence skeleton

This is something I want you to remember permanently:

> **SUBJECT → VERB → OBJECT/COMPLEMENT → EXTRA INFORMATION**

For example:

> **I** → **am** → **a student** → **at college**

> **Rahul** → **plays** → **cricket** → **every evening**

> **She** → **became** → **angry** → **after the argument**

Once you can see this skeleton, topics like **tenses, subject-verb agreement, clauses, conditionals, sentence correction, active/passive voice, direct/indirect speech** become much easier.

---

# 26. One very important warning

Don't confuse:

> **sentence**

with

> **subject + verb**

A sentence can be much bigger.

For example:

> Although I was extremely tired after studying for five hours, I continued studying because my exam was approaching.

That's still **one sentence**.

But it contains multiple clauses and many phrases.

So when we study grammar, we'll gradually learn to **break a big sentence into pieces.**

That's the skill you ultimately need for verbal ability.
