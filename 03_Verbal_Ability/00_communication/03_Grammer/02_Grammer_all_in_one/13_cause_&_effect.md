# Cause & Effect in English Grammar

**Cause and Effect** is a very important concept for **verbal ability, sentence completion, conjunctions, sentence rearrangement, reading comprehension, and logical reasoning**.

The basic idea is extremely simple:

> **CAUSE = Why something happens**
> **EFFECT = What happens because of it**

---

# 1. What is a Cause?

A **cause** is the reason something happens.

Ask:

> **Why did it happen?**

### Example

> It was raining heavily.

This can be a **cause**.

Why?

Because it can lead to something else:

> The match was cancelled.

So:

**Heavy rain = Cause**

---

# 2. What is an Effect?

An **effect** is the result of something happening.

Ask:

> **What happened because of it?**

Example:

> It was raining heavily, so the match was cancelled.

Here:

* **Cause:** It was raining heavily.
* **Effect:** The match was cancelled.

---

# 3. The Basic Pattern

The most important structure is:

> **CAUSE → EFFECT**

For example:

> He studied hard → He passed the exam.

```text
He studied hard
      ↓
    CAUSE
      ↓
He passed the exam
      ↓
    EFFECT
```

---

# 4. Cause and Effect Can Be Reversed in a Sentence

The logical relationship stays the same even if the order changes.

### Cause first

> **Because he studied hard**, he passed the exam.

Cause → Effect

### Effect first

> He passed the exam **because he studied hard**.

Effect → Cause

Both mean essentially the same thing.

# 6. BECAUSE

**Because** introduces a reason/cause.

Structure:

> **because + subject + verb**

Example:

> He stayed home **because he was sick**.

Cause:

> he was sick

Effect:

> he stayed home

### Important

After **because**, use a **clause**.

> because **he was sick**

Not:

> ❌ because his sickness

For a noun/noun phrase, use **because of**.

---

# 7. BECAUSE OF

**Because of** is followed by a **noun/noun phrase/gerund**.

> The match was cancelled **because of the rain**.

Cause:

> the rain

Compare:

> The match was cancelled **because it was raining**.

**because + clause**

> The match was cancelled **because of the rain**.

**because of + noun phrase**

This distinction is very important in error-detection questions.

---

# 8. BECAUSE vs BECAUSE OF

| Because                  | Because of                   |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------- |
| followed by clause       | followed by noun/noun phrase |
| because + subject + verb | because of + noun            |
| because he was ill       | because of his illness       |
| because it rained        | because of the rain          |

### Example

> He couldn't attend **because he was sick**. ✅

> He couldn't attend **because of his illness**. ✅

> He couldn't attend **because of he was sick**. ❌

---

# 9. SINCE

**Since** can show cause.

> **Since** it was raining, we stayed home.

Meaning:

> Because it was raining, we stayed home.

But remember that **since** has another meaning:

> I have lived here **since 2020**.

Here, "since" refers to **time**, not cause.

So:

> Since = **because** OR **from a particular time**

Context determines the meaning.

---

# 10. AS

**As** can also show cause.

> **As** he was tired, he went to bed.

= Because he was tired, he went to bed.

But **as** has several other meanings too:

> Do it **as** I told you.
> → manner

> **As** I was walking, I saw him.
> → while/time

So don't automatically assume "as" means cause.

---

# 11. DUE TO

**Due to** means **because of**.

> The flight was delayed **due to bad weather**.

Cause:

> bad weather

Effect:

> flight was delayed

Generally:

> **due to + noun/noun phrase**

---

# 12. OWING TO

**Owing to** also means **because of**.

> The train was late **owing to heavy rain**.

Same basic idea:

> owing to = because of

---

# 13. THANKS TO

**Thanks to** indicates a cause that usually produces a **positive result**.

> **Thanks to** your help, I passed the exam.

Cause:

> your help

Effect:

> I passed the exam.

It can sometimes be used sarcastically:

> Thanks to his careless driving, we missed the train.

But normally it suggests a beneficial cause.

---

# 14. ON ACCOUNT OF

Means:

> because of

Example:

> The match was cancelled **on account of heavy rain**.

It is more formal.

---

# 15. AS A RESULT OF

Means:

> because of / due to

> The roads were closed **as a result of the flood**.

Cause:

> the flood

Effect:

> roads were closed

---

# 16. SO

**So** introduces a result/effect.

> It was raining, **so** we stayed home.

Cause:

> It was raining.

Effect:

> We stayed home.

Think:

> **because → cause**

> **so → effect**

Compare:

> We stayed home **because it was raining**.

> It was raining, **so we stayed home**.

---

# 17. THEREFORE

**Therefore** means:

> for that reason / as a result

> He studied hard; **therefore**, he passed.

Cause:

> He studied hard.

Effect:

> He passed.

It is usually more formal than "so."

---

# 18. THUS

**Thus** can mean:

> therefore / as a result / in this way

Example:

> He worked hard and **thus** succeeded.

Meaning:

> He worked hard, and as a result, he succeeded.

---

# 19. HENCE

**Hence** also means:

> therefore / for this reason

> He was ill; **hence**, he didn't attend the meeting.

Cause:

> He was ill.

Effect:

> He didn't attend.

"Hence" is relatively formal.

---

# 20. CONSEQUENTLY

**Consequently** means:

> as a result

> He didn't study. **Consequently**, he failed the exam.

Cause:

> He didn't study.

Effect:

> He failed.

---

# 21. AS A RESULT

Very straightforward:

> It rained heavily. **As a result**, the roads flooded.

Cause:

> heavy rain

Effect:

> roads flooded

---

# 22. THAT'S WHY

This is more conversational.

> I was sick. **That's why** I didn't come.

Cause:

> I was sick.

Effect:

> I didn't come.

---

# 23. RESULT IN

This is an extremely important structure.

> **Cause + result in + Effect**

Example:

> Lack of sleep **results in** poor concentration.

Cause:

> lack of sleep

Effect:

> poor concentration

Other examples:

> Smoking **results in** health problems.

> Heavy rain **resulted in** flooding.

---

# 24. RESULT FROM

This works in the opposite direction.

> **Effect + result from + Cause**

Example:

> Poor concentration **results from** lack of sleep.

Effect:

> poor concentration

Cause:

> lack of sleep

So remember:

### RESULT IN

> Cause → result in → Effect

### RESULT FROM

> Effect → result from → Cause

This is a **very important exam distinction**.

---

# 25. LEAD TO

**Lead to** means:

> cause / result in

Structure:

> **Cause + lead to + Effect**

Example:

> Lack of exercise **leads to** health problems.

Cause:

> lack of exercise

Effect:

> health problems

Another:

> Hard work **leads to** success.

---

# 26. LEAD FROM?

Be careful.

The normal cause-effect expression is:

> **lead to**

Not:

> ❌ lead from

For the reverse relationship, use:

> result from

Example:

> Stress **leads to** health problems.

> Health problems **result from** stress.

---

# 27. CAUSE

"Cause" itself can function as a verb.

> Smoking **causes** lung damage.

Cause:

> smoking

Effect:

> lung damage

Structure:

> **Cause + causes + Effect**

---

# 28. GIVE RISE TO

A formal expression meaning:

> cause

> Pollution **gives rise to** health problems.

Cause:

> pollution

Effect:

> health problems

---

# 29. BRING ABOUT

Means:

> cause something to happen

> The new policy **brought about** significant changes.

Cause:

> new policy

Effect:

> significant changes

---

# 30. TRIGGER

"Trigger" means cause something to happen, often suddenly.

> The announcement **triggered** a protest.

Cause:

> announcement

Effect:

> protest

---

# 31. PROMPT

"Prompt" can mean cause or make someone do something.

> The incident **prompted** the government to take action.

Cause:

> incident

Effect:

> government took action

---

# 32. WHY

**Why** asks about the cause/reason.

> Why did he leave?

Answer:

> Because he was tired.

So:

> **Why → asks for cause**

---

# 33. REASON

"Reason" refers to a cause.

> What was the **reason** for his absence?

Answer:

> He was sick.

Therefore:

> reason = cause

---

# 34. EFFECT / RESULT / CONSEQUENCE

These generally refer to what happens because of something.

### Effect

> The effect of pollution is poor air quality.

### Result

> The result of hard work is success.

### Consequence

> The consequence of breaking the rules was punishment.

All three can refer to an outcome, although **consequence** often emphasizes a significant or negative result.

---

# 35. Cause vs Effect Vocabulary

Learn this table:

| Cause side   | Effect side  |
| ------------ | ------------ |
| because      | therefore    |
| since        | so           |
| as           | thus         |
| because of   | hence        |
| due to       | consequently |
| owing to     | as a result  |
| thanks to    | that's why   |
| reason       | result       |
| cause        | effect       |
| result in    | result from  |
| lead to      | caused by    |
| give rise to | consequence  |
| bring about  | outcome      |

---

# 36. "Caused By"

This is another important reverse structure.

> The accident was **caused by** careless driving.

Effect:

> accident

Cause:

> careless driving

Structure:

> **Effect + be caused by + Cause**

Compare:

> Careless driving **caused** the accident.

and:

> The accident was **caused by** careless driving.

Same relationship, different structure.

---

# 37. "Due to" vs "Because"

Remember:

> **because + clause**

> **because of + noun**

> **due to + noun**

Examples:

> The match was cancelled **because it rained**.

> The match was cancelled **because of the rain**.

> The match was cancelled **due to the rain**.

---

# 38. "Therefore" vs "Because"

They work in opposite directions.

### Because → introduces cause

> He failed **because he didn't study**.

### Therefore → introduces effect

> He didn't study; **therefore**, he failed.

Think:

```text
BECAUSE
Effect ← Cause

THEREFORE
Cause → Effect
```

---

# 39. Cause and Effect with Conjunctions

Many conjunctions express cause/effect relationships.

### Cause conjunctions

* because
* since
* as

### Result conjunctions/structures

* so
* so...that
* such...that

---

# 40. SO...THAT

This expresses a result.

> He was **so tired that** he fell asleep.

Cause:

> He was very tired.

Effect:

> He fell asleep.

Structure:

> **so + adjective/adverb + that + result**

Examples:

> It was **so cold that** we stayed inside.

> He ran **so quickly that** nobody could catch him.

---

# 41. SUCH...THAT

Also expresses result.

Structure:

> **such + noun phrase + that + result**

Example:

> It was **such a difficult exam that** many students failed.

Cause:

> such a difficult exam

Effect:

> many students failed.

Compare:

> It was **so difficult that** many students failed.

> It was **such a difficult exam that** many students failed.

---

# 42. Because vs So

Don't normally combine them incorrectly.

> ❌ Because it was raining, so we stayed home.

In standard grammar, use either:

> Because it was raining, we stayed home. ✅

OR:

> It was raining, so we stayed home. ✅

Not both together in the same basic construction.

---

# 43. Cause and Effect in Passive Voice

The relationship remains the same.

### Active

> Heavy rain **caused** flooding.

Cause:

> heavy rain

Effect:

> flooding

### Passive

> Flooding **was caused by** heavy rain.

Effect:

> flooding

Cause:

> heavy rain

The voice changes, but the cause-effect relationship doesn't.

---

# 44. Multiple Causes

One effect can have several causes.

Example:

> The accident happened because of **heavy rain and poor visibility**.

Causes:

1. heavy rain
2. poor visibility

Effect:

> accident

---

# 45. One Cause Can Have Multiple Effects

Example:

> Heavy rain caused **flooding, traffic jams, and power cuts**.

Cause:

> heavy rain

Effects:

1. flooding
2. traffic jams
3. power cuts

---

# 46. Chain of Cause and Effect

This is very important for **reading comprehension and para jumbles**.

Sometimes:

> A causes B.

Then:

> B causes C.

Then:

> C causes D.

Example:

```text
Heavy rain
    ↓
Flooding
    ↓
Road closures
    ↓
Traffic jams
    ↓
People arrive late
```

Here:

* Heavy rain = cause of flooding
* Flooding = effect of rain
* But flooding = cause of road closures
* Road closures = cause of traffic jams
* Traffic jams = cause of late arrival

So **one event can be both an effect and a cause**.

This is extremely important.

---

# 47. Example of a Full Chain

> Heavy rainfall caused flooding. The flooding resulted in road closures. Consequently, traffic increased, causing many people to arrive late.

You can break it down:

```text
Heavy rainfall
      ↓
   flooding
      ↓
road closures
      ↓
traffic increase
      ↓
late arrival
```

---

# 48. Cause and Effect in Para Jumbles

Suppose you have:

**A. Therefore, many people were unable to reach work.**
**B. Heavy rainfall caused severe flooding.**
**C. Several major roads were closed.**

Correct order:

> B → C → A

Why?

```text
Heavy rainfall
      ↓
flooding
      ↓
roads closed
      ↓
people couldn't reach work
```

Words like:

> therefore
> consequently
> as a result
> hence
> thus

often indicate that the sentence is describing an **effect**, so look for the cause before it.

---

# 49. Cause and Effect in Sentence Rearrangement

Suppose:

> A. As a result, crop production decreased.
> B. There was very little rainfall that year.
> C. Farmers faced severe water shortages.

Logical order:

> **B → C → A**

Because:

```text
little rainfall
      ↓
water shortage
      ↓
crop production decreases
```

This is how you can use logic rather than guessing.

---

# 50. Cause and Effect in Reading Comprehension

If a passage says:

> "The company reduced prices because sales had declined."

You should understand:

**Cause:**

> sales declined

**Effect:**

> company reduced prices

Then if a question asks:

> Why did the company reduce prices?

Answer:

> Because sales had declined.

---

# 51. Cause vs Correlation

This is slightly more advanced but useful.

Just because two things happen together doesn't necessarily mean one caused the other.

Example:

> Ice cream sales increase when temperature increases.

Both are related, but the deeper factor is:

> **hot weather**

Hot weather can cause:

> more ice cream consumption

So don't automatically assume:

> A happens with B → A caused B.

---

# 52. Necessary Cause vs Sufficient Cause

For higher-level reasoning:

### Necessary cause

Something that is required for an outcome.

Example:

> Oxygen is necessary for ordinary combustion.

Without oxygen, ordinary combustion cannot continue.

### Sufficient cause

Something that is enough to produce an outcome under the relevant conditions.

You generally won't need this distinction for basic grammar, but it can appear in reasoning questions.

# 54. Very Important Direction Table

Memorize this:

| Expression   | Direction      |
| ------------ | -------------- |
| cause        | Cause → Effect |
| lead to      | Cause → Effect |
| result in    | Cause → Effect |
| give rise to | Cause → Effect |
| bring about  | Cause → Effect |
| trigger      | Cause → Effect |
| produce      | Cause → Effect |
| result from  | Effect ← Cause |
| arise from   | Effect ← Cause |
| stem from    | Effect ← Cause |
| caused by    | Effect ← Cause |
| due to       | Effect ← Cause |

Example:

> Stress **leads to** poor sleep.

**Stress → poor sleep**

But:

> Poor sleep **results from** stress.

**Poor sleep ← stress**

# 55. A Simple Formula to Remember Everything

### If you see:

> **because / since / as**

Look for the **cause**.

### If you see:

> **therefore / so / thus / hence / consequently**

Look for the **effect**.

### If you see:

> **lead to / result in / cause**

The thing **before** it is usually the cause.

### If you see:

> **result from / caused by / due to / because of**

The thing **after** it is usually the cause.

### Example 1

> He was late **because of traffic**.

Cause = traffic

---

### Example 2

> He was late, **so** he missed the bus.

Cause = he was late

---

### Example 3

> Lack of sleep **leads to** poor concentration.

Cause = lack of sleep
Effect = poor concentration

---

### Example 4

> Poor concentration **results from** lack of sleep.

Effect = poor concentration
Cause = lack of sleep

---

### Example 5

> The flight was delayed **due to bad weather**.

Cause = bad weather
Effect = flight delay

---

### Example 6

> He didn't study; **therefore**, he failed.

Cause = didn't study

# 57. The Most Important Exam Rules

For your verbal ability preparation, these are the rules I would memorize:

### Rule 1

> **because + clause**

> because **he was sick**

### Rule 2

> **because of + noun**

> because of **his illness**

### Rule 3

> **due to + noun**

> due to **heavy rain**

### Rule 4

> **so → result/effect**

> It rained, **so** we stayed home.

### Rule 5

> **therefore → result/effect**

> He studied; **therefore**, he passed.

### Rule 6

> **result in = cause → effect**

> Exercise results in better health.

### Rule 7

> **result from = effect → cause**

> Better health results from exercise.

### Rule 8

> **lead to = cause → effect**

> Exercise leads to better health.

### Rule 9

> **caused by = effect → cause**

> The accident was caused by careless driving.

### Rule 10

> Don't use **because + so** together in a normal cause-effect sentence.

> ❌ Because it rained, so we stayed home.

> ✅ Because it rained, we stayed home.

> ✅ It rained, so we stayed home.

### The one thing you should always think about

Whenever you see a cause-and-effect question, **don't just look for keywords**.

First ask:

> **"What happened first/reason-wise?" → CAUSE**

Then:

> **"What happened because of it?" → EFFECT**

That prevents mistakes even when the question doesn't contain obvious words like *because*, *therefore*, or *so*.
