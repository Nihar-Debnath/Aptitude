No. **Infinitive** and **infinity** are different words. In grammar, the correct term is **infinitive**.

### 1. Infinitive

An **infinitive** is a form of a verb, usually:

> **to + V1 (base form)**

Examples:

* **to eat**
* **to go**
* **to study**
* **to play**
* **to learn**
* **to write**

Here:

> to + eat → **to eat** = infinitive
> to + go → **to go** = infinitive

### 2. What does "infinity" mean?

**Infinity** is a mathematical concept meaning something that has **no end or limit**.

Example:

> 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, ... → continues toward **infinity**

So **infinity has nothing to do with the grammatical term infinitive**.

---

# What exactly is an infinitive?

There are actually **two important forms** you should know.

### A. To-infinitive

> **to + V1**

Examples:

> I want **to study**.

> She decided **to leave**.

> He likes **to play** football.

> We need **to practice**.

Here, **to study, to leave, to play, to practice** are infinitives.

---

### B. Bare infinitive

Sometimes the **"to" is omitted**.

This is called a **bare infinitive**.

> **V1 without "to"**

For example:

> I can **swim**.

Here **swim** is a bare infinitive.

Why?

Because after a modal verb like **can**, we use the base verb:

> can + swim
> can + go
> can + study

Not:

> can to swim ❌

Other examples:

> You **should study**.

> He **must leave**.

> They **will come**.

So:

| Type                | Structure | Example  |
| ------------------- | --------- | -------- |
| **To-infinitive**   | to + V1   | to study |
| **Bare infinitive** | V1        | study    |

---

# Why is it called an "infinitive"?

Because it is **not limited by person or number**.

Look at:

> I **study**.
> He **studies**.
> They **study**.

The finite verb changes according to the subject.

But:

> I want **to study**.
> He wants **to study**.
> They want **to study**.

**to study** stays the same.

That's one reason the infinitive is considered a **non-finite verb form**.

---

# Infinitive vs finite verb

This is important because you've already been studying **finite and non-finite verbs**.

### Finite

> She **works** every day.

**works** changes according to the subject:

> I work.
> She works.

So **works** is finite.

### Infinitive

> She wants **to work**.

**to work** doesn't change:

> I want to work.
> She wants to work.
> They want to work.

So **to work** is a **non-finite infinitive**.

---

# Infinitive can do different jobs

An infinitive can behave somewhat like a **noun, adjective, or adverb** in a sentence.

### As a noun

> **To learn** is important.

What is important?

→ **To learn**

### As an adjective

> I have a book **to read**.

Which book?

→ a book **to read**

### As an adverb

> I went to the library **to study**.

Why did I go?

→ **to study**

---

## Very important distinction

Don't confuse these three:

| Word           | Meaning                                      |
| -------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| **Infinitive** | Grammar term: `to + V1` or bare V1           |
| **Infinite**   | Without limit/end                            |
| **Infinity**   | Mathematical idea of something without limit |

So when talking about grammar, say:

> **infinitive** ✅

Not:

> **infinity** ❌

And since you're studying **non-finite verbs**, remember:

> **Infinitive is one type of non-finite verb.**
> The other major types are **gerunds** and **participles**.
