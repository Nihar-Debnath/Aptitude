Absolutely. **Prepositions** are one of the most important grammar topics for verbal ability because they appear everywhere in **error spotting, sentence correction, fill-in-the-blanks, cloze tests, and sentence improvement**.

Let's build it from **absolute zero**.

# 1. What is a Preposition?

A **preposition is a word that shows the relationship between a noun/pronoun and another word in the sentence.**

That sounds complicated, so look at examples:

> The book is **on** the table.

`on` tells us the relationship between:

**book ↔ table**

> She is **in** the room.

`in` tells us the relationship between:

**she ↔ room**

> I went **to** school.

`to` shows the relationship between:

**went ↔ school**

So think:

> **Preposition = relationship word**

---

# 2. The easiest way to understand prepositions

Imagine:

> 🐱 CAT
> 📦 BOX

Now:

> The cat is **in** the box.

`in` tells you the cat's relationship with the box.

Change it:

> The cat is **on** the box.

> The cat is **under** the box.

> The cat is **behind** the box.

> The cat is **near** the box.

The cat and box didn't change.

Only the **relationship** changed.

That's what prepositions do.

# 5. Prepositions of PLACE

Let's start with the most important ones.

## IN

`In` means **inside an area/container/place**.

> The keys are **in** the box.

> She is **in** the room.

> He lives **in** Kolkata.

> There is water **in** the bottle.

Think:

> **inside**

---

# 6. ON

`On` generally means **touching a surface**.

> The book is **on** the table.

> The phone is **on** the bed.

> There is a picture **on** the wall.

Think:

> **surface**

---

# 7. AT

`At` generally points to a **specific point/location**.

> He is **at** the station.

> She is **at** the door.

> I am **at** school.

Think:

> **point**

So:

**IN → inside/area**

**ON → surface**

**AT → point**

---

# 8. IN vs ON vs AT

Imagine a city:

> I live **in** Kolkata.

Now a street:

> I live **on** Park Street.

Now a specific location:

> I am **at** 25 Park Street.

This is a useful conceptual model:

> **IN → larger area**

> **ON → surface/street/line**

> **AT → specific point**

But these are not absolute rules; usage depends on context.

---

# 9. Prepositions of TIME

This is extremely important for exams.

The three most important are:

> **at, on, in**

---

# 10. AT + specific time

> at 5 PM

> at noon

> at midnight

> at night

Examples:

> I wake up **at 6 AM**.

> The train arrives **at noon**.

---

# 11. ON + day/date

> on Monday

> on Friday

> on 15 August

> on my birthday

> on Monday morning

Examples:

> I will meet him **on Monday**.

> The exam is **on 15 August**.

---

# 12. IN + larger period

> in August

> in 2026

> in winter

> in the morning

> in the evening

> in the 21st century

Think:

> **AT → exact point**

> **ON → day/date**

> **IN → longer period**

---

# 13. The famous AT / ON / IN table

| Preposition | Time                              |
| ----------- | --------------------------------- |
| **at**      | 5 PM, noon, midnight, night       |
| **on**      | Monday, Sunday, 15 August         |
| **in**      | August, 2026, winter, the morning |

Examples:

> at 7 PM

> on Monday

> in August

This is one of the first things you should memorize.

---

# 14. FROM

`From` indicates the **starting point**.

> I came **from** Kolkata.

> The train runs **from** Delhi.

> I work **from** Monday.

Usually:

> **from A to B**

> from Kolkata **to** Delhi

> from Monday **to** Friday

---

# 15. TO

`To` commonly shows:

### Destination

> I went **to** school.

### Direction

> He walked **to** the station.

### Relationship/range

> from 5 **to** 10

---

# 16. FOR

`For` commonly indicates **duration** or purpose.

### Duration

> I waited **for two hours**.

> He lived there **for five years**.

### Purpose

> This medicine is **for** headaches.

> This gift is **for** you.

---

# 17. SINCE

`Since` indicates the **starting point of a period that continues until now/another reference point**.

> I have lived here **since 2020**.

`2020` = starting point.

Compare:

> I have lived here **for six years**.

`Six years` = duration.

So:

### Since → starting point

> since Monday

> since 2020

> since morning

### For → duration

> for two days

> for six years

> for three hours

---

# 18. SINCE vs FOR

This is a very common exam question.

> I have been studying **since 8 AM**. ✅

8 AM = starting point

> I have been studying **for three hours**. ✅

Three hours = duration

Don't say:

> since three hours ❌

---

# 19. BY

`By` can have several meanings.

### Deadline

> Finish the work **by Monday**.

Meaning:

**no later than Monday**

### Method/means

> I travelled **by train**.

> Send it **by email**.

### Agent in passive voice

> The book was written **by** Shakespeare.

---

# 20. UNTIL / TILL

Means **up to a particular time**.

> Wait **until 5 PM**.

> I worked **till midnight**.

So:

> by 5 PM = deadline

> until 5 PM = continuing up to that time

Compare:

> Finish the work **by 5 PM**.

You can finish at 2, 3, 4, or 5.

> Stay here **until 5 PM**.

You remain here up to 5.

---

# 21. BETWEEN

`Between` is generally used for **two** people/things/points.

> The house is **between** the school and the hospital.

> Divide the money **between** Ram and Shyam.

Traditional exam rule:

> **between → two**

---

# 22. AMONG

`Among` is generally used for **more than two**.

> He was sitting **among** his friends.

> Divide the money **among** the five students.

Traditional exam rule:

> **between → two**

> **among → more than two**

But there's a more advanced point: `between` can also be used with more than two when talking about distinct individual relationships.

For your basic exam preparation, remember the traditional rule first.

---

# 23. BESIDE vs BESIDES

Very common trap.

### BESIDE

Means **next to**.

> He sat **beside** me.

### BESIDES

Means **in addition to**.

> Besides English, I study mathematics.

So:

> beside = next to

> besides = in addition to

---

# 24. ABOVE vs OVER

Both can mean higher than something.

> The fan is **above** my head.

> The plane flew **over** the city.

Generally:

### ABOVE

Higher position, not necessarily movement/contact.

### OVER

Can indicate directly above, covering, or movement across.

> Put a blanket **over** the child.

> The bird flew **over** the house.

---

# 25. BELOW vs UNDER

### BELOW

Lower than something.

> The temperature is **below** zero.

### UNDER

Usually directly beneath, often with a sense of covering/physical position.

> The cat is **under** the table.

---

# 26. THROUGH vs ACROSS

This is important.

### THROUGH

Movement **inside and from one side to another**.

> We walked **through** the forest.

Imagine entering the forest and moving inside it.

### ACROSS

Movement from **one side to the other**, usually across a surface/open area.

> We walked **across** the road.

> He swam **across** the river.

Think:

> through = inside

> across = from one side to another

---

# 27. INTO vs IN

Very important.

### IN

Position.

> He is **in** the room.

He's already there.

### INTO

Movement toward the inside.

> He went **into** the room.

He moved from outside → inside.

So:

> in = position

> into = movement

---

# 28. ON vs ONTO

Same idea.

### ON

Position:

> The book is **on** the table.

### ONTO

Movement toward a surface:

> He jumped **onto** the table.

---

# 29. WITH

`With` can show:

### Accompaniment

> I went **with** my friend.

### Instrument

> Cut it **with** a knife.

### Possession/feature

> A man **with** a beard.

### Manner

> He spoke **with** confidence.

---

# 30. BY vs WITH

This is VERY important in passive sentences.

> The novel was written **by** Shakespeare.

`by` → person who performed the action.

> The window was broken **with** a stone.

`with` → instrument used.

So:

**BY → agent/person**

**WITH → instrument/tool**

---

# 31. WITHOUT

Means absence of something.

> He left **without** his phone.

> She completed the work **without** help.

---

# 32. AGAINST

Means opposition or contact with something.

> He is **against** the proposal.

> The ladder is **against** the wall.

---

# 33. ABOUT

Can mean:

### Topic

> We talked **about** the exam.

### Approximately

> There were **about** 100 people.

---

# 34. OF

One of the most common prepositions.

It can show:

### Possession/relationship

> the roof **of** the house

### Material/content

> a cup **of** tea

### Part/quantity

> a piece **of** cake

### Characteristic

> a man **of** courage

# 35. Common preposition combinations

This is where prepositions become **very important for competitive exams**.

You can't always translate prepositions logically.

English has fixed combinations.

For example:

> interested **in**

> good **at**

> afraid **of**

> proud **of**

> angry **with**

> depend **on**

> belong **to**

> listen **to**

> look **at**

These have to be learned as combinations.

# 36. Adjective + Preposition

Since you just learned adjectives, connect the topics.

Some common combinations:

| Adjective   | Preposition |
| ----------- | ----------- |
| good        | at          |
| bad         | at          |
| interested  | in          |
| afraid      | of          |
| proud       | of          |
| fond        | of          |
| capable     | of          |
| famous      | for         |
| responsible | for         |
| different   | from        |
| similar     | to          |
| married     | to          |
| dependent   | on          |
| angry       | with/at     |
| pleased     | with        |
| satisfied   | with        |

Examples:

> He is good **at** mathematics.

> She is interested **in** music.

> I am proud **of** you.

> He is responsible **for** the project.

# 37. Verb + Preposition

Many verbs have fixed prepositions.

### Depend on

> It depends **on** you.

### Listen to

> Listen **to** me.

### Wait for

> I am waiting **for** you.

### Look at

> Look **at** this.

### Belong to

> This book belongs **to** me.

### Apologize to someone

> I apologized **to** him.

### Apply for

> I applied **for** the job.

These are extremely useful in sentence correction.

---

# 38. Noun + Preposition

Nouns can also have fixed combinations.

> reason **for**

> solution **to**

> answer **to**

> need **for**

> demand **for**

> access **to**

> interest **in**

Examples:

> What is the reason **for** your absence?

> We need a solution **to** this problem.

> He has access **to** the database.

---

# 39. The BIG exam problem: wrong preposition

A sentence can be grammatically correct except for one preposition.

Example:

> He is good **in** mathematics. ❌

> He is good **at** mathematics. ✅

Another:

> She is married **with** a doctor. ❌

> She is married **to** a doctor. ✅

Another:

> We discussed **about** the problem. ❌

> We discussed the problem. ✅

This last one is especially important.

---

# 40. Some verbs DON'T need a preposition

This is a major source of errors.

### Discuss

Incorrect:

> We discussed **about** the issue. ❌

Correct:

> We discussed the issue. ✅

Because `discuss` already takes a direct object.

### Enter

Incorrect:

> He entered **into** the room. ❌ *(in standard exam usage when simply meaning entered the room)*

Correct:

> He entered the room. ✅

### Reach

Incorrect:

> We reached **at** the station. ❌

Correct:

> We reached the station. ✅

### Request

Incorrect:

> He requested **for** help. ❌

Correct:

> He requested help. ✅

These are excellent error-spotting traps.

---

# 41. IN vs INTO — exam perspective

> He is **in** the room. ✅

Position.

> He went **into** the room. ✅

Movement.

But in everyday English, `in` can sometimes be used for movement:

> He went in the room.

However, for formal grammar questions, **into** clearly expresses movement into an enclosed space.

---

# 42. AT vs IN for places

Compare:

> He is **at** the school.

Focus = location/point.

> He is **in** the school.

Focus = inside the building.

Similarly:

> at the hospital

> in the hospital

Context can change the meaning.

---

# 43. ON time vs IN time

Very important.

### ON TIME

Exactly at the scheduled time.

> The train arrived **on time**.

### IN TIME

Before it is too late.

> We reached the station **in time** to catch the train.

Think:

**on time = punctual**

**in time = before deadline**

---

# 44. BY vs UNTIL

Again:

> Submit the form **by Friday**.

Deadline.

> Stay here **until Friday**.

Continuation.

This distinction appears frequently in exams.

---

# 45. DURING vs FOR

### DURING

Used with an event/period.

> during the meeting

> during the summer

> during the exam

### FOR

Used with duration.

> for two hours

> for three days

> for five years

Incorrect:

> during two hours ❌ *(when expressing duration)*

Correct:

> for two hours ✅

---

# 46. SINCE vs FROM

They can both indicate a starting point, but they aren't interchangeable in all contexts.

### Since

Usually connects a starting point with a period continuing until now/another reference time.

> I have lived here **since 2020**.

### From

Simply indicates the starting point/range.

> The shop is open **from 9 AM to 9 PM**.

---

# 47. PREPOSITIONAL PHRASE

This is an important concept.

A **preposition + its object** forms a **prepositional phrase**.

Example:

> The book is **on the table**.

`on` = preposition

`the table` = object of preposition

Together:

> **on the table** = prepositional phrase

Another:

> He lives **in Kolkata**.

`in Kolkata` = prepositional phrase.

---

# 48. Object of a preposition

The noun/pronoun after the preposition is called its **object**.

> on **the table**

`on` = preposition

`table` = object

> with **him**

`with` = preposition

`him` = object

Notice:

> with **him** ✅

not:

> with **he** ❌

Because after a preposition, we generally use the **object form** of a pronoun.

---

# 49. Pronouns after prepositions

Remember:

| Subject | Object |
| ------- | ------ |
| I       | me     |
| he      | him    |
| she     | her    |
| we      | us     |
| they    | them   |
| who     | whom   |

Examples:

> between you and **me** ✅

Not:

> between you and I ❌

Because `between` is a preposition.

---

# 50. Preposition at the end of a sentence

This is something many traditional grammar books make confusing.

It is perfectly normal in modern English for a preposition to appear at the end.

> Who are you talking **to**?

> What are you looking **at**?

> This is the house I live **in**.

These are natural English.

For competitive exams, however, the expected structure may depend on the particular sentence and exam style. Don't assume "preposition at the end = always wrong."

---

# 51. Preposition vs conjunction

This is an important distinction.

Consider:

> I stayed home **because of** the rain.

`because of` = preposition phrase
followed by noun:

> the rain

But:

> I stayed home **because** it was raining.

`because` = conjunction
followed by a clause:

> it was raining

So:

**Preposition → usually followed by noun/pronoun/noun phrase**

**Conjunction → connects clauses**

---

# 52. Preposition vs adverb

Sometimes the same word can function differently.

> He went **inside**.

`inside` = adverb.

> He went **inside the house**.

`inside` = preposition.

Why?

Because now it has an object:

> inside **the house**

This is why you should identify a word by **how it functions in the sentence**, not just memorize a fixed list.

---

# 53. Simple, compound, and phrase prepositions

### Simple prepositions

One word:

> in, on, at, by, for, from, to, with

### Compound prepositions

Words formed/used as units:

> inside, outside, beneath, beyond, throughout

### Prepositional phrases

Multiple words functioning as a preposition:

> because of

> in front of

> in spite of

> instead of

> according to

> due to

> on behalf of

Examples:

> He stayed home **because of** the rain.

> He stood **in front of** me.

---

# 54. Important compound/phrase prepositions

Learn these:

> because of = due to a reason

> in spite of = despite

> instead of = in place of

> according to = as stated by

> in front of = before/at the front

> on behalf of = representing

> by means of = using

> with regard to = concerning

---

# 55. IN SPITE OF vs DESPITE

Both mean **although something happened / despite a difficulty**.

> In spite **of** the rain, we played.

> Despite the rain, we played.

Important:

> despite **of** ❌

Correct:

> despite the rain ✅

But:

> in spite **of** the rain ✅

---

# 56. BECAUSE vs BECAUSE OF

### Because

Followed by a clause.

> We stayed home **because it was raining**.

### Because of

Followed by a noun/noun phrase.

> We stayed home **because of the rain**.

This distinction is extremely important.

---

# 57. DUE TO vs BECAUSE OF

Both can express cause.

> The match was cancelled **because of** rain.

> The cancellation was **due to** rain.

In strict formal grammar, `due to` traditionally modifies a noun and `because of` modifies a verb/clause, although modern usage is more flexible.

For exams, remember the traditional distinction if your syllabus tests it.

---

# 58. Prepositions after "different"

Traditional exam grammar often expects:

> different **from** ✅

Example:

> Your answer is different **from** mine.

You may encounter `different than` in some varieties of English, but **different from** is the safest choice for formal competitive exams.

# 60. Very important: superior/inferior

Don't say:

> superior than ❌

> inferior than ❌

Correct:

> superior **to** ✅

> inferior **to** ✅

Similarly:

> senior **to**

> junior **to**

Example:

> He is senior **to** me.

Not:

> He is senior than me. ❌

This is a classic competitive-exam trap.

---

# 61. "Prefer" — another famous trap

Traditional exam grammar:

> I prefer tea **to** coffee.

Not:

> I prefer tea than coffee. ❌

Structure:

> **prefer A to B**

---

# 62. "Consist" — another famous trap

> The team consists **of** eleven players.

Not:

> consists from ❌

So:

> consist **of**

---

# 63. "Comprise" — be careful

Traditional exam questions often test:

> The whole comprises ten parts.

`comprise` already means "consists of/includes."

So avoid:

> comprises of ❌

Use:

> comprises ten parts ✅

or:

> consists of ten parts ✅

---

# 64. "Married"

> He is married **to** her. ✅

Not:

> married with her ❌

---

# 65. "Good at"

> She is good **at** mathematics.

Not:

> good in mathematics ❌

Although `good in` can occur in other meanings/contexts, **good at** is the standard ability construction.

---

# 66. "Angry with" vs "angry at"

Both can occur.

> angry **with** a person

> angry **at** a situation/action

Examples:

> She was angry **with me**.

> He was angry **at the decision**.

---

# 67. Preposition and gerund

This is very important.

After a preposition, if you use a verb, it generally takes the **-ing form**.

> He is interested **in learning** English.

Not:

> interested in learn ❌

> She left without **saying** goodbye.

Not:

> without say ❌

> I am tired of **waiting**.

Not:

> tired of wait ❌

Why?

Because after a preposition, a verb generally becomes a **gerund**.

---

# 68. This creates a famous trap

> I look forward to **meeting** you. ✅

Not:

> I look forward to meet you. ❌

Why?

Because here `to` is a **preposition**, not the infinitive marker.

Therefore:

> to + noun/gerund

> look forward to **meeting**

---

# 69. But "to" can also be an infinitive marker

Compare:

> I want **to study**.

Here `to` + base verb = infinitive.

But:

> I am committed **to studying**.

Here `to` = preposition.

Therefore:

> to study ✅

> committed to studying ✅

This distinction becomes very important in advanced error spotting.

---

# 70. The complete mental model

When you see a preposition, think:

### 1. What relationship does it show?

Place? Time? Direction? Cause? Method?

### 2. What is its object?

> in **the room**

### 3. Is it part of a fixed combination?

> good **at**

> interested **in**

> depend **on**

### 4. Is the next word a noun/pronoun/gerund?

> interested in **music**

> interested in **learning**

### 5. Is this a verb that doesn't take a preposition?

> discuss the issue

> reach the station

> enter the room

## 🧠 The simplest definition to remember

If you want one sentence for your notes:

> **A preposition is a word that shows the relationship of a noun/pronoun (or its equivalent) with another word in a sentence.**

And the most important idea is:

> **Preposition + object = prepositional phrase**

Examples:

> **in** the room

> **on** the table

> **with** my friend

> **after** the exam

> **because of** the rain

Once you understand this, the next level is **preposition rules and fixed combinations**—because that's where most of the actual **error-spotting questions** come from.
