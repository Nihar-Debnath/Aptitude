Yes — **"degree" in grammar is mainly used for adjectives and adverbs**, because it tells us the **level or intensity of a quality**.

Let's make this very clear.

# 1. What does "degree" mean?

Here, **degree = level of comparison/intensity**.

Look at:

> Ram is **tall**.

Now compare Ram with Amit:

> Ram is **taller** than Amit.

Now compare Ram with everyone in the class:

> Ram is **the tallest** student in the class.

The quality is the same:

> **tall**

But its **degree** changes.

So:

| Degree      | Example | Meaning               |
| ----------- | ------- | --------------------- |
| Positive    | tall    | quality exists        |
| Comparative | taller  | higher than another   |
| Superlative | tallest | highest among a group |

---

# 2. Positive Degree

Don't let the word **positive** confuse you.

It doesn't mean "good."

It simply means the **basic form** of the adjective.

> tall
> beautiful
> intelligent
> good
> difficult

Examples:

> Ram is **tall**.

> This book is **interesting**.

No comparison is necessary.

---

# 3. Comparative Degree

**Comparative = comparing two.**

Example:

> Ram is **taller than** Amit.

We're comparing:

**Ram ↔ Amit**

Another:

> This book is **more interesting than** that book.

So comparative generally means:

> **A > B**

Examples:

> tall → taller

> fast → faster

> beautiful → more beautiful

> difficult → more difficult

---

# 4. Superlative Degree

**Superlative = highest/lowest degree among a group.**

Example:

> Ram is **the tallest** student in the class.

We're comparing Ram against the other students.

Conceptually:

> Ram > everyone else

Other examples:

> the fastest

> the biggest

> the most beautiful

> the most difficult

---

# 5. Why do we call them "degrees"?

Think about a thermometer.

You can have:

> 20°C → 30°C → 40°C

The **level** increases.

Similarly:

> tall → taller → tallest

The **degree of the quality** increases.

It's basically asking:

> **How much of that quality?**

---

# 6. Does degree apply only to adjectives?

### Not exactly.

**Adjectives AND adverbs can have degrees of comparison.**

### Adjective

> He is **fast**.

> He is **faster** than me.

> He is the **fastest** runner.

### Adverb

> He runs **fast**.

> He runs **faster** than me.

> He runs the **fastest**.

Here:

`fast` is an adjective in the first set and an adverb in the second.

Both can have comparison.

---

# 7. Adjective vs adverb degrees

### Adjective

Describes a **noun/pronoun**.

> Rahul is **smart**.

`smart` → adjective

Comparison:

> Rahul is **smarter** than Amit.

> Rahul is the **smartest** student.

### Adverb

Describes a **verb/action**.

> Rahul runs **quickly**.

`quickly` → adverb

Comparison:

> Rahul runs **more quickly** than Amit.

> Rahul runs the **most quickly**.

So **degree isn't a separate part of speech**.

It's a **grammatical property/form of comparison** that adjectives and some adverbs can have.

---

# 8. Very important: Not every adjective has degrees

This is where things get interesting.

Some adjectives naturally describe qualities that can be compared:

> tall → taller → tallest

> beautiful → more beautiful → most beautiful

But some adjectives are normally **absolute/non-gradable**.

For example:

> dead

Something is generally either dead or not dead.

We don't normally say:

> deader ❌

> deadest ❌

Similarly:

> unique

Traditional grammar considers:

> more unique ❌

> most unique ❌

because something is either unique or it isn't.

Other examples often treated this way:

> perfect

> impossible

> complete

> entire

> final

In formal/exam English, avoid treating these as normally gradable.

---

# 9. But there is another concept: gradable adjectives

Some adjectives can have **different levels**.

For example:

> cold

Something can be:

> a little cold
> quite cold
> very cold
> extremely cold

So `cold` is **gradable**.

Likewise:

> big
> small
> beautiful
> expensive
> difficult

These can exist at different levels.

---

# 10. "Very" shows degree too

Look:

> He is **cold**.

> He is **very cold**.

> He is **extremely cold**.

The adjective hasn't changed form, but its **degree/intensity** has changed.

So there are actually two ideas you should distinguish:

### Degree of comparison

> tall → taller → tallest

### Degree/intensity

> tall → very tall → extremely tall

For your competitive-exam grammar, when a chapter says **"Degrees of Comparison"**, it usually means:

> **Positive → Comparative → Superlative**

---

# 11. A very important distinction

Look at:

> Ram is **tall**.

This is **positive degree**.

> Ram is **very tall**.

Still **positive degree**.

Why?

Because we're not comparing Ram with anybody.

`very` only increases the intensity.

But:

> Ram is **taller than Amit**.

Now it's **comparative degree**.

And:

> Ram is **the tallest** boy in the class.

Now it's **superlative degree**.

So:

| Sentence                         | Degree      |
| -------------------------------- | ----------- |
| Ram is tall.                     | Positive    |
| Ram is very tall.                | Positive    |
| Ram is taller than Amit.         | Comparative |
| Ram is the tallest in the class. | Superlative |

---

# 12. One more important thing

**Positive degree does NOT mean "positive/good."**

For example:

> He is **bad**.

`bad` is still **positive degree**.

Then:

> He is **worse** than me.

Comparative.

> He is the **worst** player.

Superlative.

So:

> bad → worse → worst

Even though `bad` has a negative meaning, its grammatical degree is **positive**.

---

# 13. Your mental model

Whenever you hear:

> **Degree of an adjective**

Think:

### "At what level is this quality being expressed or compared?"

For example:

**GOOD**

> good → basic level
> better → higher than another
> best → highest among a group

**TALL**

> tall → basic
> taller → higher
> tallest → highest

**BEAUTIFUL**

> beautiful → basic
> more beautiful → higher
> most beautiful → highest

---

## 🔥 Final takeaway

**Degree is not another type of adjective.**

It's a **form/level of comparison**.

And the traditional three degrees are:

> **Positive → Comparative → Superlative**

And yes, **adverbs can also have degrees of comparison**, not just adjectives.

So when you're studying adjectives, **Degrees of Comparison is one of the major topics inside adjectives**, and we'll eventually need to learn all the rules for forming comparative/superlative forms and all the tricky exam cases.

Yes. If you already know the **basic degree of adjectives**—Positive, Comparative, Superlative—then for **verbal ability**, you should go deeper into the rules because questions often test **comparison, sentence correction, error spotting, and usage**.

Let's build it properly from basic → exam level.

# 1. What exactly is "Degree"?

**Degree of comparison** tells us the **level/extent of a quality** possessed by someone or something.

Example:

* Ram is **tall**. → Positive
* Ram is **taller** than Amit. → Comparative
* Ram is **the tallest** boy in the class. → Superlative

There are **3 degrees**:

| Degree      | Purpose                | Example |
| ----------- | ---------------------- | ------- |
| Positive    | Describes quality      | tall    |
| Comparative | Compares **2**         | taller  |
| Superlative | Compares **3 or more** | tallest |

But verbal ability goes much deeper than simply knowing these three forms.

---

# 2. Positive Degree

Positive doesn't necessarily mean "good."

It simply means the **basic form of the adjective**.

> Rahul is **tall**.

> This is a **beautiful** house.

> She is **intelligent**.

There is **no comparison** in the normal positive form.

### Important

Don't think:

> Positive = good
> Comparative = better
> Superlative = best

That's incorrect.

"Positive" means the adjective is in its **base form**.

---

# 3. Comparative Degree

Comparative is normally used when comparing **two** people/things.

> Rahul is **taller than** Amit.

> Gold is **more expensive than** silver.

### The most important word:

**than**

Comparative + **than** is extremely common.

❌ Rahul is taller Amit.
✅ Rahul is taller **than** Amit.

---

# 4. Superlative Degree

Superlative normally compares **one against a group of three or more**.

> Rahul is **the tallest** boy in the class.

Notice:

**the + superlative**

* the tallest
* the fastest
* the smartest
* the most beautiful

❌ Rahul is tallest boy in the class.
✅ Rahul is **the tallest** boy in the class.

---

# 5. The BIG verbal-ability rule: 2 vs 3+

This is one of the most important concepts.

### Two people/things → Comparative

> Rahul is taller than Amit.

### Three or more → Superlative

> Rahul is the tallest of the three boys.

But there's an important exception/nuance.

If you are comparing **two**, don't normally use superlative.

❌ Rahul is the tallest of the two.

Better:

✅ Rahul is the taller of the two.

This is a **very common exam rule**.

---

# 6. "Of the two" → Comparative

Memorize this.

> He is **the better of the two**.

> She is **the taller of the two sisters**.

> This is **the more interesting of the two books**.

Why?

Because there are only **two** things being compared.

Therefore:

**the + comparative + of the two**

---

# 7. "Than any other" → Comparative

This is another huge verbal-ability pattern.

Look at:

> Rahul is taller than any other boy in the class.

This means Rahul is taller than **every other boy**.

We use:

**comparative + than any other + singular noun**

Example:

> Mount Everest is higher than any other mountain in the world.

This can be converted into:

> Mount Everest is **the highest mountain in the world**.

So:

### Comparative

> Everest is higher than any other mountain.

### Superlative

> Everest is the highest mountain.

These two can express essentially the same idea.

---

# 8. "Than all other" → Comparative

You may also see:

> Rahul is taller than all other boys in the class.

Notice:

**all other + plural noun**

This is also correct.

Compare:

> Rahul is taller than **any other boy**.

> Rahul is taller than **all other boys**.

Both can mean Rahul is taller than everyone else.

---

# 9. "One of the" + Superlative

This is extremely important.

After:

**one of the + superlative**

we normally use a **plural noun**.

✅ He is one of the **best players**.

✅ She is one of the **smartest students**.

❌ He is one of the best **player**.

Why plural?

Because you're saying he is **one member of a group**.

> one of the best **players**

means:

> He is among the group of best players.

---

# 10. "One of the most..." structure

Very common in exams:

> Kolkata is one of the **largest cities** in India.

> Sachin is one of the **greatest cricketers** in history.

> This is one of the **most difficult questions**.

Pattern:

**one of the + superlative + plural noun**

---

# 11. "The + comparative, the + comparative"

This is a special comparative structure.

Example:

> **The more you study, the more you learn.**

Meaning:

As studying increases, learning increases.

Another:

> **The harder you work, the better you become.**

Structure:

**The + comparative ..., the + comparative ...**

Examples:

> The faster you run, the more tired you become.

> The more you practice, the better you perform.

> The less you sleep, the less productive you become.

This is frequently tested in sentence correction.

---

# 12. Double comparatives

This is usually wrong.

❌ He is **more taller** than me.

Why?

Because **taller** already contains the comparative idea.

You don't normally use:

> more + taller

Correct:

✅ He is **taller** than me.

Similarly:

❌ more better
❌ more faster
❌ more easier

Correct:

✅ better
✅ faster
✅ easier

---

# 13. But "more" + adjective is sometimes correct

For longer adjectives:

> beautiful → more beautiful

> intelligent → more intelligent

> difficult → more difficult

So:

❌ beautifuller
❌ intelligent-er

Correct:

✅ more beautiful
✅ more intelligent

---

# 14. "Less" is also comparative

People often forget this.

> Rahul is **less intelligent than** Amit.

This is a comparison.

**less + adjective + than**

Examples:

> This book is less expensive than that one.

> He is less experienced than his brother.

So comparative doesn't always mean "more."

It can mean **less** too.

---

# 15. "Least" is superlative

Similarly:

> He is the **least intelligent** student in the class.

> This is the **least expensive** option.

Structure:

**the least + adjective**

---

# 16. Positive Degree can also make comparisons

This is one of the most important deeper concepts.

You can compare two things using:

**as + adjective + as**

Example:

> Rahul is **as tall as** Amit.

This is called a **positive-degree comparison**.

It means:

> Rahul and Amit have equal height.

### Negative

> Rahul is **not as tall as** Amit.

Meaning:

> Rahul is shorter than Amit.

This is extremely important for sentence transformation.

---

# 17. "As...as" structure

Memorize:

**as + adjective + as**

Examples:

> He is as intelligent as his brother.

> She is as beautiful as her sister.

> This car is as expensive as that car.

---

# 18. "Not as...as"

This indicates inequality.

> Rahul is not as tall as Amit.

Equivalent:

> Amit is taller than Rahul.

So:

### Positive structure

> Rahul is as tall as Amit.

### Negative

> Rahul is not as tall as Amit.

### Comparative equivalent

> Amit is taller than Rahul.

---

# 19. "So...as"

In traditional grammar/exam questions, **so + adjective + as** is commonly used in negative comparisons.

> He is not so intelligent as his brother.

Equivalent:

> He is not as intelligent as his brother.

For modern English, **not as...as** is generally more natural.

But in grammar exams, you may encounter:

> not so...as

So recognize it.

---

# 20. Irregular degrees

These are VERY important for verbal ability.

Some adjectives don't follow normal `-er/-est` rules.

| Positive | Comparative     | Superlative       |
| -------- | --------------- | ----------------- |
| good     | better          | best              |
| bad      | worse           | worst             |
| little   | less            | least             |
| much     | more            | most              |
| many     | more            | most              |
| far      | farther/further | farthest/furthest |
| old      | older/elder     | oldest/eldest     |

You should memorize these.

---

# 21. "Farther" vs "Further"

This can appear in exams.

### Farther

Usually refers to **physical distance**.

> Kolkata is farther from Delhi than Patna is.

### Further

Usually means **additional/more**.

> For further information, contact the office.

> We need further discussion.

But modern English sometimes uses **further** for physical distance too.

For competitive exams, remember the traditional distinction.

---

# 22. "Older" vs "Elder"

This is another important one.

### Older

General comparison.

> Rahul is older than Amit.

### Elder

Usually used for **family relationships** and normally before a noun.

> My elder brother is a doctor.

> Her elder sister lives in Delhi.

A common exam trap:

❌ He is elder than me.

Traditional grammar prefers:

✅ He is older than me.

And:

✅ He is my elder brother.

So remember:

**elder → family relationship + before noun**

---

# 23. "Elder" doesn't normally take "than"

Traditional exam rule:

❌ He is elder than his brother.

✅ He is older than his brother.

But:

✅ He is my elder brother.

---

# 24. "Less" vs "Lesser"

These aren't simply interchangeable.

### Less

Used for amount/degree:

> He has less money than me.

> This problem is less difficult.

### Lesser

Means **smaller/lower in importance, rank, etc.**

> He is a lesser-known writer.

> The lesser evil.

For verbal exams, don't automatically replace one with the other.

---

# 25. "Senior", "junior", "superior", "inferior"

VERY important.

These words are already comparative in meaning.

Therefore, traditionally they take **to**, NOT **than**.

❌ He is senior than me.

✅ He is senior **to** me.

❌ This product is superior than that one.

✅ This product is superior **to** that one.

Remember:

| Word      | Correct |
| --------- | ------- |
| senior    | to      |
| junior    | to      |
| superior  | to      |
| inferior  | to      |
| prior     | to      |
| posterior | to      |

This is a classic sentence-correction question.

---

# 26. "Prefer" also uses "to"

This is technically not degree, but it gets tested with comparison structures.

❌ I prefer tea than coffee.

✅ I prefer tea **to** coffee.

> I prefer reading to watching TV.

Remember:

**prefer A to B**

Not:

**prefer A than B**

---

# 27. "Unique" and absolute adjectives

Some adjectives have an **absolute meaning**.

Examples:

* unique
* perfect
* complete
* impossible
* dead
* final
* universal

Traditional grammar says these shouldn't normally be graded.

Therefore:

❌ very unique
❌ more unique
❌ most unique

Traditional exam answer:

✅ unique

Similarly:

❌ more perfect
❌ most perfect

But here's an important real-English nuance:

Modern English **does** sometimes use "very unique," "more unique," etc., especially when treating these words as gradable in ordinary usage.

For competitive exams, however, the **traditional rule** may be tested.

---

# 28. "Very" vs "much"

Another common trap.

### Positive adjective

> very good

> very beautiful

### Comparative adjective

Usually:

> much better

> much faster

> much more beautiful

Not normally:

❌ very better
❌ very faster

So:

**very + positive**

**much + comparative**

Example:

> He is **very intelligent**.

> He is **much more intelligent** than his brother.

---

# 29. "Much" + comparative

You can strengthen a comparative with:

* much
* far
* a lot
* considerably
* significantly
* even

Examples:

> He is **much taller** than me.

> This is **far better**.

> She is **considerably more experienced**.

> This method is **much more efficient**.

---

# 30. "By far" + superlative

Very common.

> He is **by far the best** player.

> This is **by far the most difficult** question.

Structure:

**by far + the + superlative**

---

# 31. "Any" vs "any other"

This is a classic error.

Consider:

> Rahul is taller than **any other boy** in the class.

Why **other**?

Because Rahul himself is a boy in the class.

We're comparing Rahul with everyone **else**.

Without "other":

> Rahul is taller than any boy in the class.

This can logically include Rahul himself, creating a problem.

So for the intended "Rahul is tallest" meaning:

✅ Rahul is taller than **any other boy**.

---

# 32. "No other" + comparative

Another very important transformation:

> No other boy in the class is as tall as Rahul.

Equivalent:

> Rahul is taller than any other boy in the class.

Equivalent:

> Rahul is the tallest boy in the class.

So these three structures are connected:

### Structure 1

**No other + noun + as + adjective + as**

> No other boy is as tall as Rahul.

### Structure 2

**Comparative + than any other**

> Rahul is taller than any other boy.

### Structure 3

**Superlative**

> Rahul is the tallest boy.

This is a BIG topic for sentence transformation.

---

# 33. Transformation: Positive → Comparative → Superlative

Let's take:

> No other city in India is as large as Mumbai.

Comparative:

> Mumbai is larger than any other city in India.

Superlative:

> Mumbai is the largest city in India.

Same fundamental meaning.

You should learn to recognize these as **equivalent structures**.

---

# 34. Another transformation

Positive:

> No other student in the class is as intelligent as Rahul.

Comparative:

> Rahul is more intelligent than any other student in the class.

Superlative:

> Rahul is the most intelligent student in the class.

This is extremely useful in:

* sentence transformation
* sentence correction
* fill in the blanks
* verbal ability
* error spotting

---

# 35. "One of the..." transformation

> Rahul is one of the best students in the class.

This does **NOT** mean Rahul is necessarily the single best student.

It means Rahul belongs to the group of students who are among the best.

So don't confuse:

> Rahul is **the best student**.

with:

> Rahul is **one of the best students**.

The first identifies one top student.

The second identifies membership in a top group.

---

# 36. "Very few" + positive degree

Another important pattern:

> Very few cities are as large as Mumbai.

Equivalent:

> Mumbai is larger than most other cities.

And often:

> Mumbai is one of the largest cities.

This structure is especially useful in transformation questions.

---

# 37. Important patterns to memorize

For verbal ability, these patterns are worth memorizing:

### Pattern 1

**A + is + comparative + than + B**

> A is taller than B.

### Pattern 2

**A + is + as + adjective + as + B**

> A is as tall as B.

### Pattern 3

**A + is + not as + adjective + as + B**

> A is not as tall as B.

### Pattern 4

**A + is + the + superlative + ...**

> A is the tallest boy.

### Pattern 5

**A + is + comparative + than any other + singular noun**

> A is taller than any other boy.

### Pattern 6

**No other + singular noun + is as + adjective + as + A**

> No other boy is as tall as A.

### Pattern 7

**One of the + superlative + plural noun**

> one of the tallest boys

### Pattern 8

**the + comparative + of the two**

> the taller of the two

### Pattern 9

**the + comparative, the + comparative**

> The more you study, the better you perform.

### Pattern 10

**senior/junior/superior/inferior + to**

> He is senior to me.

---

# 38. A very important exam distinction: "than me" vs "than I"

You may see:

> He is taller than me.

This is completely normal in modern English.

Traditional grammar may prefer:

> He is taller than I am.

Why?

Because the full comparison is:

> He is taller than I am tall.

Obviously we don't say the entire thing, so:

> He is taller than I am.

For verbal exams, **"than I am"** is often the safest formal construction.

But don't assume **"than me" is always grammatically wrong**. It is standard in ordinary modern English.

---

# 39. "The more..., the more..." is NOT ordinary superlative

Look carefully:

> The **more** you study, the **better** you become.

"more" and "better" are comparative forms.

The word **the** here does not mean superlative.

This is a special **correlative comparative** structure.

That's a common conceptual trap.

Yes — **correlative comparative constructions are related to Degree of Comparison**, but they are a **special comparison structure**, not a separate "4th degree."

And you're right to question it. When you're studying **Degree deeply for verbal ability**, I should include these related constructions.

Let's organize the whole thing properly.

# 1. First: What is a correlative comparative construction?

Look at:

> **The more you study, the better you become.**

There are **two comparative expressions**:

* more → comparative
* better → comparative

And they are connected:

> **The more X, the more/better Y.**

The two parts depend on each other.

That's why it's called **correlative**:

* **correlative** = two things that work together
* **comparative** = involves comparison

So:

> **The more you practice, the better you become.**

means:

> As your practice increases, your performance improves.

---

# 2. Is it part of Degree?

### YES.

But understand the hierarchy:

```text
DEGREE OF COMPARISON
│
├── Positive
│   ├── as...as
│   └── not as...as
│
├── Comparative
│   ├── -er + than
│   ├── more + adjective + than
│   ├── less + adjective + than
│   ├── comparative + than any other
│   ├── comparative + of the two
│   └── CORRELATIVE COMPARATIVE
│       └── the more..., the more...
│
└── Superlative
    ├── the + -est
    ├── the most
    ├── one of the + superlative
    └── etc.
```

So **correlative comparative = a special use of comparative degree**.

It isn't a new degree.

---

# 3. Why does it use "the"?

This confuses many students.

Look:

> **The more** you study, **the better** you become.

You might think:

> "the" = superlative?

No.

Here **more** and **better** are still **comparatives**.

The `the` is part of this special construction:

> **the + comparative..., the + comparative...**

It doesn't turn the comparative into a superlative.

---

# 4. The basic formula

Memorize:

> **The + comparative + ..., the + comparative + ...**

Examples:

> The more you study, the more you learn.

> The harder you work, the better you perform.

> The faster you run, the more tired you become.

> The less you sleep, the less productive you become.

> The older he gets, the wiser he becomes.

---

# 5. Why is it called "correlative"?

Because there are **two related changes**.

Example:

> The more you practice, the better you become.

Think:

```text
Practice ↑
     ↓
Performance ↑
```

Or:

```text
More practice → Better performance
```

Both sides change together.

That's the **correlation**.

---

# 6. There are actually TWO common meanings

### A. Positive relationship

More X → more Y

> The more you study, the more you learn.

> The harder you work, the better you perform.

### B. Negative relationship

More X → less Y

> The more you spend, the less you save.

> The more you eat, the less hungry you become.

Or:

> The less you sleep, the more tired you become.

So don't memorize only:

> the more..., the more...

The important idea is:

> **comparative + comparative relationship**

---

# 7. There is another important construction related to this

This is where your question becomes important.

If you're preparing **Degree for verbal ability**, you should know several related comparison structures.

## A. Equality

### `as + adjective + as`

> Rahul is as tall as Amit.

This is a **positive-degree comparison**.

---

## B. Inequality

### `not as + adjective + as`

> Rahul is not as tall as Amit.

Meaning:

> Amit is taller than Rahul.

---

## C. Comparative

### `-er + than`

> Rahul is taller than Amit.

---

## D. Comparative with long adjectives

### `more + adjective + than`

> Rahul is more intelligent than Amit.

---

## E. Negative comparative

### `less + adjective + than`

> Rahul is less intelligent than Amit.

---

## F. Superlative

### `the + adjective-est`

> Rahul is the tallest student.

---

## G. Superlative with long adjectives

### `the most + adjective`

> Rahul is the most intelligent student.

---

# 8. Then we have comparison transformations

These are **VERY important for verbal ability**.

Suppose:

> Rahul is the tallest boy in the class.

You should recognize:

### Superlative

> Rahul is the tallest boy.

### Comparative

> Rahul is taller than any other boy in the class.

### Positive

> No other boy in the class is as tall as Rahul.

These three can communicate essentially the same comparison.

This is a major area of **sentence transformation**.

---

# 9. "Very few" is another related structure

Example:

> Very few students in the class are as intelligent as Rahul.

This can be expressed as:

> Rahul is more intelligent than most other students in the class.

And:

> Rahul is one of the most intelligent students in the class.

So you should learn this pattern too.

### Structure:

> **Very few + plural noun + are + as + adjective + as + X**

Example:

> Very few players are as talented as Sachin.

→ Sachin is more talented than most other players.

→ Sachin is one of the most talented players.

---

# 10. "No other" is extremely important

Example:

> **No other boy is as tall as Rahul.**

This is positive degree, but it expresses the idea of a superlative.

Equivalent:

> Rahul is taller than any other boy.

Equivalent:

> Rahul is the tallest boy.

This is why you shouldn't study degree only as adjective forms.

---

# 11. "Any other" is connected to this

> Rahul is taller than **any other boy** in the class.

Here:

**any other + singular noun**

is a very important pattern.

Don't confuse it with:

> Rahul is taller than **any boy** in the class.

For the intended "Rahul is the tallest" meaning, **any other boy** is the standard construction.

---

# 12. "All other" is also related

> Rahul is taller than **all other boys** in the class.

Here:

**all other + plural noun**

Compare:

> taller than **any other boy**

vs

> taller than **all other boys**

Both can express that Rahul is taller than everyone else.

---

# 13. "Of the two" is another comparison structure

When exactly **two** things are being compared:

> Rahul is the **taller of the two**.

Not:

> ❌ Rahul is the tallest of the two.

Similarly:

> She is the better of the two candidates.

> This is the more expensive of the two phones.

So:

```text
2 → comparative
3+ → superlative
```

But specifically:

> **the + comparative + of the two**

---

# 14. "One of the..." is another important structure

> Rahul is **one of the best students** in the class.

Pattern:

> **one of the + superlative + plural noun**

Examples:

> one of the best players

> one of the most beautiful places

> one of the fastest cars

Notice:

❌ one of the best **player**

✅ one of the best **players**

---

# 15. There are also "increasing comparison" constructions

These are related to comparative degree.

Examples:

> The situation is getting **better and better**.

> He is becoming **more and more confident**.

> The problem is becoming **more and more difficult**.

Pattern:

### Short adjective

> comparative + and + comparative

> bigger and bigger

> faster and faster

### Long adjective

> more and more + adjective

> more and more difficult

This is also worth knowing for verbal ability.

---

# 16. "Less and less"

Same idea:

> He is becoming less and less interested.

> The problem is becoming less and less important.

Meaning:

The degree is continuously decreasing.

---

# 17. "Ever + comparative"

You may encounter:

> It is getting **better and better**.

or:

> The situation is becoming **increasingly difficult**.

Also:

> He is becoming **ever more ambitious**.

"Ever more" means increasingly more.

This is less important than the structures above, but you may encounter it in reading comprehension or advanced grammar.

---

# 18. Comparison with modifiers

Comparatives can be strengthened or weakened.

### Stronger

> much better

> far better

> considerably better

> significantly better

> a lot better

### Example

> This method is **much better than** the previous one.

---

### Slightly

> slightly better

> somewhat better

> a little better

Example:

> This phone is **slightly better than** the old one.

These are all connected to comparative usage.

---

# 19. "By far" + superlative

Another useful structure:

> He is **by far the best** player.

> This is **by far the most difficult** question.

Pattern:

> **by far + the + superlative**

Meaning:

There is a large difference between the winner and the others.

---

# 20. Special comparison words

These are extremely important for verbal ability because they don't follow ordinary `than` rules.

### senior

> He is senior **to** me.

### junior

> He is junior **to** me.

### superior

> This product is superior **to** that one.

### inferior

> This product is inferior **to** that one.

### prior

> This happened prior **to** the meeting.

Don't use:

❌ senior than
❌ superior than
❌ inferior than

---

# 21. "Prefer" also belongs in your comparison notes

> I prefer tea **to** coffee.

Not:

❌ I prefer tea than coffee.

And:

> I prefer reading **to** watching TV.

This isn't technically a "degree of adjective" rule, but it is closely related to **comparison structures** and is frequently tested in verbal ability.
