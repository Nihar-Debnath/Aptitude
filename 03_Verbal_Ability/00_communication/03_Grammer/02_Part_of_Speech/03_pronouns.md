Perfect. Now you're moving to the **second building block of English**.

If nouns are the **names**, then pronouns are the **substitutes** for those names.

Most books define a pronoun as:

> **"A pronoun is a word used instead of a noun."**

That definition is correct—but it's incomplete. It doesn't explain **why** English needs pronouns or **how** they work.

Let's learn pronouns the way English actually uses them.

---

# What is a Pronoun?

A **pronoun** is a word that **takes the place of a noun**.

Why?

Because repeating the same noun again and again sounds awkward.

Imagine this:

> Rahul woke up.
>
> Rahul brushed Rahul's teeth.
>
> Rahul ate Rahul's breakfast.
>
> Rahul went to Rahul's college.

Grammatically, it's mostly correct.

But no native speaker talks like this.

Instead:

> Rahul woke up.
>
> He brushed his teeth.
>
> He ate his breakfast.
>
> He went to college.

"He" and "his" replace "Rahul."

Those replacement words are **pronouns**.

---

# Why do Pronouns exist?

English likes sentences to be smooth and natural.

Pronouns help us:

* avoid repetition
* make conversations shorter
* make writing easier to read
* connect ideas between sentences

Without pronouns, English would sound robotic.

---

# Where do pronouns appear?

Almost everywhere.

## 1. As the subject

> She is studying.

Who is studying?

She.

The pronoun is doing the action.

---

## 2. As the object

> I called him.

Who received the action?

Him.

---

## 3. After prepositions

> Give it to her.

Her is the object of the preposition "to."

---

## 4. Showing possession

> This is my phone.

My tells us who owns the phone.

---

## 5. Referring back to something

> I bought a laptop.
>
> It is very fast.

"It" refers back to "laptop."

---

# Types of Pronouns

This is where grammar becomes interesting.

There are many kinds.

We'll understand each one.

---

# 1. Personal Pronouns

These are the most common.

| Subject | Object |
| ------- | ------ |
| I       | me     |
| You     | you    |
| He      | him    |
| She     | her    |
| It      | it     |
| We      | us     |
| They    | them   |

Examples

> I am tired.

> She likes pizza.

> They play football.

---

Notice something.

Wrong

> Me am tired.

Correct

> I am tired.

---

Wrong

> Him likes cricket.

Correct

> He likes cricket.

---

# Subject vs Object Pronouns

This confuses many learners.

Subject pronouns perform the action.

> She loves him.

Who loves?

She.

Subject.

Who receives the action?

Him.

Object.

Another example.

> I saw her.

Who saw?

I.

Who was seen?

Her.

---

A simple trick:

Ask yourself:

**Who is doing the action?**

→ Subject pronoun.

**Who is receiving the action?**

→ Object pronoun.

---

# 2. Possessive Pronouns

They show ownership.

Mine

Yours

His

Hers

Its

Ours

Theirs

Examples

> This book is mine.

> The bag is hers.

> The house is ours.

Notice:

Wrong

> This book is my.

Correct

> This book is mine.

---

# Possessive Adjectives

These look similar but are different.

| Possessive Adjective | Possessive Pronoun |
| -------------------- | ------------------ |
| my                   | mine               |
| your                 | yours              |
| his                  | his                |
| her                  | hers               |
| our                  | ours               |
| their                | theirs             |

Compare.

> This is my phone.

"This" modifies "phone."

But

> This phone is mine.

Now "mine" stands alone.

---

# 3. Reflexive Pronouns

Used when the subject and object are the same person.

myself

yourself

himself

herself

itself

ourselves

yourselves

themselves

Example

> I taught myself programming.

The same person did the teaching and the learning.

Another.

> She looked at herself.

---

Common mistake

Wrong

> Me myself did it.

Correct

> I did it myself.

---

# 4. Demonstrative Pronouns

They point to something.

this

that

these

those

Examples

> This is my laptop.

> Those are beautiful.

---

Difference.

This

Near

That

Far

These

Near (plural)

Those

Far (plural)

---

# 5. Interrogative Pronouns

Used to ask questions.

Who

Whom

Whose

Which

What

Examples

> Who came?

> What happened?

> Which is yours?

---

# Who vs Whom

Many learners struggle with this.

Who = subject.

> Who called you?

Whom = object.

> Whom did you call?

In modern spoken English, many native speakers use **who** instead of **whom**, but it's useful to know the formal rule.

---

# 6. Relative Pronouns

They connect two pieces of information.

who

whom

whose

which

that

Example

> The boy who won the race is my friend.

"Who won the race" gives extra information about "the boy."

Another.

> The phone that I bought is expensive.

---

# 7. Indefinite Pronouns

They don't refer to a specific person or thing.

someone

anyone

everyone

everything

nothing

something

few

many

none

Examples

> Someone is calling.

> Everybody laughed.

> Nothing happened.

---

# 8. Reciprocal Pronouns

Used when two or more people do something to one another.

each other

one another

Examples

> They help each other.

> The two friends respect each other.

---

# 9. Distributive Pronouns

Refer to members of a group individually.

each

either

neither

Examples

> Each has a ticket.

> Neither is correct.

---

# Pronouns and Antecedents

Here's an important word.

## Antecedent

The noun that the pronoun replaces.

Example

> Rahul bought a car.
>
> He loves it.

Antecedents:

He → Rahul

It → car

Without an antecedent, readers may not know what the pronoun refers to.

---

# Pronoun Agreement

Pronouns must match their antecedent.

Correct

> The girl forgot her bag.

Girl → her

Correct

> The boys forgot their bags.

Boys → their

Wrong

> The boys forgot her bags.

---

# Common Pronoun Patterns

Subject + Verb

> He runs.

---

Subject + Verb + Object

> She called him.

---

Subject + Verb + Object + Preposition

> They talked to us.

---

Possessive

> Our house is beautiful.

---

Reflexive

> I blamed myself.

# Tricky Things About Pronouns

This is where learners make mistakes.

---

## 1. I vs Me

Wrong

> Me went home.

Correct

> I went home.

---

Wrong

> She gave it to I.

Correct

> She gave it to me.

Rule:

"I" is a subject pronoun.

"Me" is an object pronoun.

---

## 2. He and Him

Wrong

> Him is my brother.

Correct

> He is my brother.

---

Wrong

> I saw he.

Correct

> I saw him.

---

## 3. They vs Them

Wrong

> Them are ready.

Correct

> They are ready.

---

Wrong

> I called they.

Correct

> I called them.

---

## 4. Your vs You're

These are often confused, but only one is a pronoun.

Your = possessive adjective

> Your phone is ringing.

You're = "you are"

> You're late.

---

## 5. Its vs It's

Another common confusion.

Its = possessive

> The dog wagged its tail.

It's = "it is" or "it has"

> It's raining.

Notice that unlike most possessives, **its** has **no apostrophe**.

---

## 6. This vs It

Compare.

> This is my new phone.

Used when introducing or pointing to something.

After that:

> It is very fast.

Don't keep saying "this" if you've already introduced the noun.

---

## 7. Everyone, Someone, Nobody

These look plural because they refer to many people, but grammatically they are singular.

> Everyone is welcome.

> Someone has arrived.

---

## 8. One

English sometimes uses **one** to mean "a person in general."

> One should always be honest.

This is formal. In everyday conversation, people often say:

> You should always be honest.

---

# Pronouns Can Replace More Than One Word

A pronoun doesn't always replace just one noun.

Example

> Eating healthy every day is difficult.
>
> It takes discipline.

"It" refers to the entire idea of eating healthy every day.

---

# How to Identify a Pronoun

Ask yourself:

1. Is this word naming something?
2. Or is it replacing something already mentioned?
3. Can I find the noun it refers to (its antecedent)?

Example:

> Sarah bought a bicycle because she wanted one.

Nouns:

* Sarah
* bicycle

Pronouns:

* she (Sarah)
* one (bicycle)

# One Important Thing Most Grammar Books Don't Tell You

A pronoun **doesn't have meaning by itself**. Its meaning comes from the noun it refers to.

Compare:

> He is sleeping.

Who is **he**?

We don't know.

Now:

> Rahul is sleeping. He is tired.

Now "he" has a clear meaning because its antecedent is "Rahul."

That's why good writing always makes it clear what a pronoun refers to.

---

# How to Practice Pronouns

Don't just memorize the lists. Train yourself to see how pronouns replace nouns.

1. Read a short paragraph.
2. Circle every pronoun.
3. Draw an arrow from each pronoun to its antecedent.
4. Rewrite the paragraph by replacing the pronouns with the original nouns. Notice how repetitive it sounds.
5. Then rewrite it again using pronouns. You'll see how much smoother English becomes.
