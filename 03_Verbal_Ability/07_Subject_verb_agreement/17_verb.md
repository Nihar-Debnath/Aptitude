Excellent question. This is actually one of the **most important topics in English grammar**.

There are **not only "have" verbs**. English has **three Primary Auxiliary (Helping) Verbs**.

1. **Be**
2. **Have**
3. **Do**

Then there are **Modal Verbs** like can, could, will, would, shall, should, may, might, must, etc.

So the complete picture is:

```
English Verbs
│
├── Main Verbs (eat, play, study, write...)
│
├── Primary Auxiliary Verbs
│     ├── Be
│     ├── Have
│     └── Do
│
└── Modal Verbs
      ├── can
      ├── could
      ├── will
      ├── would
      ├── shall
      ├── should
      ├── may
      ├── might
      ├── must
      └── ought to
```

We'll learn them one by one.

# ⭐ Golden Rule (Very Important)

Only **three verbs** change according to the subject in the **present tense**:

| Base Verb | Singular (He/She/It) | Plural (I/You/We/They) |
| --------- | -------------------- | ---------------------- |
| **be**    | is *(I → am)*        | are                    |
| **have**  | has                  | have                   |
| **do**    | does                 | do                     |

For **most other verbs**, the rule is simpler:

| Base Verb | Singular    | Plural    |
| --------- | ----------- | --------- |
| play      | **plays**   | **play**  |
| eat       | **eats**    | **eat**   |
| write     | **writes**  | **write** |
| study     | **studies** | **study** |
| go        | **goes**    | **go**    |

Examples:

* He **plays** cricket. ✅
* They **play** cricket. ✅
* She **writes** a letter. ✅
* We **write** letters. ✅

This is why **be**, **have**, and **do** are called **primary auxiliary verbs**—they have special forms that every English learner should memorize. Once you know these three and the general rule for other verbs, subject-verb agreement becomes much easier.

Yes! **This is the part that makes verbs much easier.**
Earlier we classified verbs by *what they are*, but now you're asking **“which verbs work with which subjects/persons, and where do they appear?”**

There are actually **different dimensions** of verb classification. A single verb can belong to several categories at the same time.

For example:

> **She is reading a book.**

* `reading` → main verb
* `is` → helping verb
* `is reading` → verb phrase
* `reading` → transitive verb because it has object `a book`
* `is` → finite verb
* `reading` → non-finite form
* `she` → 3rd person singular

Let's organize the whole thing properly.

---

# 1. First: PERSON — who is doing the action?

English has **3 persons**.

| Person     | Meaning                   | Pronouns          |
| ---------- | ------------------------- | ----------------- |
| 1st person | speaker                   | I, we             |
| 2nd person | person spoken to          | you               |
| 3rd person | person/thing spoken about | he, she, it, they |

### 1st person

The speaker is doing the action:

> **I** play football.
> **We** play football.

### 2nd person

The person you're talking to:

> **You** play football.

### 3rd person

Someone/something else:

> **He** plays football.
> **She** plays football.
> **Rahul** plays football.
> **They** play football.

---

# 2. PERSON + NUMBER

Person also has **number**.

### Singular

One:

> I
> you
> he
> she
> it

### Plural

More than one:

> we
> you
> they

So:

|            | Singular  | Plural |
| ---------- | --------- | ------ |
| 1st person | I         | we     |
| 2nd person | you       | you    |
| 3rd person | he/she/it | they   |

---

# 3. The BIG rule: Subject–Verb Agreement

This is probably what you were asking about.

The **subject determines the form of the verb** in many situations.

For the **simple present tense**:

### I / We / You / They → base verb

> I **play**.
> We **play**.
> You **play**.
> They **play**.

### He / She / It → verb + s/es

> He **plays**.
> She **plays**.
> It **works**.

This is extremely important.

### Compare:

> I **work** here.
> He **works** here.

> They **go** there.
> She **goes** there.

> We **watch** TV.
> He **watches** TV.

---

# 4. But this does NOT happen with every tense

This is where beginners often get confused.

The `s/es` rule mainly applies to the **simple present**.

For example:

### Simple present

> I **play**.
> He **plays**.

### Simple past

Both:

> I **played**.
> He **played**.

No `s`.

### Simple future

Both:

> I **will play**.
> He **will play**.

Again, no `s`.

So don't memorize:

> "He always takes s."

❌ Wrong.

Instead:

> **He/she/it takes s/es in the simple present when the verb is the main verb.**

That's much more accurate.

# 5. Helping verbs have their OWN subject rules

This is very important.

Different helping verbs work with different subjects.

## BE

Forms:

> am
> is
> are
> was
> were

### Present

| Subject     | BE      |
| ----------- | ------- |
| I           | **am**  |
| he/she/it   | **is**  |
| you/we/they | **are** |

Examples:

> I **am** studying.
> He **is** studying.
> They **are** studying.

### Past

| Subject     | BE       |
| ----------- | -------- |
| I/he/she/it | **was**  |
| you/we/they | **were** |

Examples:

> I **was** studying.
> He **was** studying.
> They **were** studying.

# 6. HAVE has subject rules too

Present:

> have
> has

### I / we / you / they → HAVE

> I **have** a car.

> They **have** a car.

### he / she / it → HAS

> He **has** a car.
> She **has** a car.

Everyone uses:

> **had**

> I had
> He had
> They had

Present:

> do / does

### I / we / you / they → DO

> I **do** my work.
> They **do** their work.

### he / she / it → DOES

> He **does** his work.
> She **does** her work.

Past:

Everyone:

> **did**

> I did.

# 8. Modal verbs are much easier

This is one of the beautiful things about **modal auxiliaries**.

After a modal:

> can, could, may, might, shall, should, will, would, must

the verb stays in its **base form**.

It doesn't matter who the subject is.

### CAN

> I **can swim**.
> He **can swim**.
> She **can swim**.
> They **can swim**.

❌ He can swims.

❌ She can swims.

Correct:

> He **can swim**.

---

### WILL

> I **will go**.
> He **will go**.
> They **will go**.

Not:

> ❌ He will goes.

---

### SHOULD

> I should study.
> He should study.
> They should study.

Always:

> **modal + base verb**

---

# 9. Where do modal verbs go?

Normally:

> **Subject + Modal + Base Verb**

Examples:

> She **can swim**.

> They **should study**.

> He **must leave**.

> We **will win**.

> You **may go**.

---

# 10. What about questions?

The helping/modal verb moves before the subject.

### Statement

> She **can swim**.

### Question

> **Can she swim?**

Structure:

> **Modal + subject + base verb?**

Examples:

> Can you help me?

> Should I go?

> Will he come?

> Could they do it?

---

# 11. What about negative sentences?

Put **not** after the auxiliary/modal.

> She **is not** sleeping.

> He **does not** work here.

> They **cannot** swim.

> You **should not** go.

Structure:

> Subject + auxiliary/modal + not + verb

---

# 12. Main verb vs helping verb: who works with whom?

A **helping verb works together with a main verb**.

For example:

> She **is studying**.

`is` = helping verb
`studying` = main verb

Together:

> **is studying** = verb phrase

Another:

> He **has finished** his work.

`has` = helping verb
`finished` = main verb

Another:

> They **will come** tomorrow.

`will` = modal auxiliary
`come` = main verb

---

# 13. The important combinations

You should remember these:

### BE + V-ing

> is running
> are studying
> was sleeping
> were playing

Examples:

> He **is running**.

> They **are playing**.

---

### HAVE + V3

> has eaten
> have gone
> had finished

Examples:

> She **has eaten**.

> They **have finished**.

---

### DO + V1

> do work
> does work
> did work

Examples:

> I **do work** hard.

> He **does work** hard.

> They **did work** hard.

But normally in positive sentences, we don't need `do`; it is especially common for **questions, negatives, or emphasis**:

> Does he **work** here?

> He does not **work** here.

---

### MODAL + V1

> can go
> should study
> will come
> must leave

Examples:

> He **can swim**.

> She **should study**.

# 14. Transitive and intransitive verbs — "who works with whom?"

This classification is about **objects**, not persons.

### Transitive verb

The action goes to an object.

> She **opened** the door.

`opened` → transitive
`door` → object

Ask:

> Opened **what?**

Answer:

> the door

Therefore transitive.

---

### Intransitive verb

No object is required.

> He **slept**.

You cannot naturally ask:

> Slept what?

So `slept` is intransitive here.

---

# 15. A verb can be BOTH transitive and intransitive

This is an important point.

For example:

> She **runs** every morning.

No object → intransitive.

But:

> She **runs** a company.

Object = `a company` → transitive.

So don't memorize:

> "run = intransitive."

Instead:

> **A verb's transitivity can depend on how it is used in the sentence.**

---

# 16. Linking verbs work differently

A linking verb doesn't show an action going to an object.

It **links the subject to information about the subject**.

Common linking verbs:

> be, seem, become, appear, feel, look, smell, taste, sound, remain, stay

Example:

> She **is** happy.

`is` links:

> She ↔ happy

`happy` describes the subject.

Another:

> He **became** angry.

`angry` describes the subject `he`.

Another:

> The food **smells** delicious.

`delicious` describes the food.

---

# 17. Linking verb vs transitive verb

This is a common exam trap.

Compare:

> He **looks** tired.

`looks` = linking verb
`tired` = describes `he`

But:

> He **looks at** the picture.

Here `looks at` is an action/verb phrase involving an object.

Similarly:

> The soup **tastes** good.

Linking.

But:

> She **tasted** the soup.

Transitive.

So **context matters**.

---

# 18. Finite verbs — the verb connected to the subject/tense

A finite verb is a verb form that is tied to **tense and/or subject agreement**.

Examples:

> He **plays** football.

`plays` = finite.

Why?

Because it changes according to subject:

> I play.
> He plays.

And it can show tense:

> He played.
> He plays.

---

# 19. Non-finite verbs don't change according to person

Examples:

> to play
> playing
> played

depending on usage.

Look at:

> I want **to play**.

> He wants **to play**.

`to play` doesn't become:

> ❌ to plays

So `to play` is non-finite.

Similarly:

> I enjoy **playing**.

> He enjoys **playing**.

`playing` doesn't change.

---

# 20. Regular vs irregular — this is about past forms

This classification has **nothing to do with person**.

### Regular

Usually add `-ed`:

> play → played → played

> work → worked → worked

> walk → walked → walked

### Irregular

Past form changes differently:

> go → went → gone

> eat → ate → eaten

> see → saw → seen

> write → wrote → written

---

# 21. Phrasal verbs

A phrasal verb is generally:

> **Verb + particle**

Examples:

> give up
> look after
> turn off
> wake up
> find out
> look for

Examples:

> He **gave up** smoking.

> She **looks after** her brother.

> Turn **off** the light.

And phrasal verbs can also be transitive/intransitive.

### Intransitive

> Wake up!

No object.

### Transitive

> Turn **off** the light.

Object = `the light`.

# 23. Another example

> **They will have finished the work.**

`They` → 3rd person plural

`will` → modal auxiliary

`have` → auxiliary

`finished` → main verb, past participle

`the work` → object

Think about verbs as having **different questions asked about them**.

### Question 1: What is the verb doing?

**Main / Helping / Linking**

> She **is** happy.
> She **is studying**.

---

### Question 2: Does it need an object?

**Transitive / Intransitive**

> She opened **the door**. → transitive

> She slept. → intransitive

---

### Question 3: Does its past form follow the normal pattern?

**Regular / Irregular**

> work → worked → regular

> go → went → irregular

---

### Question 4: Is it tied to tense/subject?

**Finite / Non-finite**

> He **plays** → finite

> He likes **to play** → non-finite `to play`

---

### Question 5: Does it express modality?

**Modal**

> can, could, may, might, shall, should, will, would, must

---

### Question 6: Is it a verb + particle combination?

**Phrasal verb**

> give up
> look after
> turn off

| ------- | -------------- | --- | ---- | ---- | -------- |
| I       | play           | am  | have | do   | can play |
| You     | play           | are | have | do   | can play |
| He      | plays          | is  | has  | does | can play |
| She     | plays          | is  | has  | does | can play |
| It      | plays          | is  | has  | does | can play |
| We      | play           | are | have | do   | can play |

> **He/she/it → s/es, is, has, does**

> **I/we/you/they → base verb, am/are, have, do**

> I can
> he can
> she can

## The most important thing to understand

Don't think these 11 categories are **11 separate types that never overlap**.

They are **different ways of describing the same verb**.

For example:

> **He has written a letter.**

`has` = auxiliary + finite + 3rd-person singular

`written` = main verb + non-finite + irregular verb's V3

And `written` is part of a **transitive construction** because:

> written **what?** → a letter

So one sentence can contain **many classifications at once**.

That is the missing "who works with whom" picture.
