# ✅ Rule 9: **One of + Plural Noun + Singular Verb**

## The Rule

The structure is:

```text
One of + plural noun + singular verb
```

Even though the noun after **of** is **plural**, the **subject is "one"**, which is singular.

---

## Formula

```text
One of + plural noun
        ↓
Subject = One
        ↓
Singular verb
```

---

## Examples ✅

* **One of the students is** absent.
* **One of my friends works** at TCS.
* **One of the books has** gone missing.
* **One of the players was** injured.

---

## Why?

Take this sentence:

> **One of the students is absent.**

Many students see **students** and think:

> Students = plural → **are** ❌

But ask yourself:

### Who is absent?

* All students? ❌
* **One student?** ✅

So the subject is **One**, not **students**.

Therefore:

✅ **is**

---

```text

↑
Real Subject
```

Ignore **students**.

Focus on **One**.

# Don't Confuse These

## 1. One of...

> **One of the students is** absent.

Only **one** student.

---

## 2. All of...

> **All of the students are** absent.

Now the subject is **all**.

Plural.

---

## 3. Some of...

Depends on the noun.

> **Some of the students are** absent.

Plural noun → **are**

> **Some of the water is** dirty.

Uncountable noun → **is**

# The "One of the..." vs. "The Only One of the..." Trap 🚨

This is an advanced rule that appears in competitive exams.

### Example

> **One of the students who are preparing for TCS is from Kolkata.**

Here:

* **who are preparing** → refers to **students** (plural)
* **is from Kolkata** → refers to **one** (singular)

✅ Correct.

---

Now look at this:

> **The only one of the students who is preparing for TCS is from Kolkata.**

Now **who** refers to **the only one**.

So:

✅ **is preparing**

This is a higher-level rule, but it's good to know.

Excellent question. This is exactly how you should learn grammar—by looking for **patterns**, not isolated rules.

The answer is:

> **No. "Neither of" does NOT come under Rule 9.**

It belongs to the **same family of singular expressions**, but it's a different pattern.

Let's organize them.

---

# Family 1: **One of + Plural Noun**

Pattern:

```text
One of + plural noun + singular verb
```

Examples:

* One of the students **is** absent.
* One of my friends **works** at Infosys.
* One of the books **has** disappeared.

---

# Family 2: **Neither of / Either of**

These also take **singular verbs**.

### Pattern

```text
Neither of + plural noun + singular verb
```

```text
Either of + plural noun + singular verb
```

### Examples

* **Neither of the boys is** ready. ✅
* **Neither of the answers is** correct. ✅
* **Either of the roads leads** to the station. ✅
* **Either of the candidates has** enough experience. ✅

### Why?

Because:

* **Neither** = **Not one**
* **Either** = **One of the two**

So the subject is singular.

---

# Family 3: **Each of / Every one of**

### Pattern

```text
Each of + plural noun + singular verb
```

Examples:

* **Each of the students has** an ID card.
* **Each of the players is** fit.

---

### Pattern

```text
Every one of + plural noun + singular verb
```

Examples:

* **Every one of the books is** useful.
* **Every one of the employees has** completed the training.

> ⚠️ **Every one of** (two words) is different from **everyone** (one word), but **both take singular verbs**.

---

# Family 4: **None of**

This one is special.

Traditionally:

* **None of the students is** absent. ✅

Modern English also accepts:

* **None of the students are** absent. ✅

### For placement exams:

👉 Prefer **is** unless the exam clearly expects modern usage.

---

# Family 5: **Any of / Some of / Most of / All of**

These **do not** always take singular verbs.

The verb depends on the noun that follows.

### Examples

* **Some of the students are** absent.

* **Some of the water is** dirty.

* **Most of the books are** interesting.

* **Most of the money is** gone.

* **All of the players are** ready.

* **All of the milk is** spoiled.

# Rule

For these words:

* Some of
* Most of
* All of
* Any of

**Ignore "some", "most", "all", "any".**

Look only at the noun after **of**.

### Formula

```text
Some of + plural noun
↓

Plural verb

Some of + uncountable noun
↓

Singular verb
```

---

# Examples

### Some of

* Some of the books **are** new.
* Some of the sugar **is** wet.

---

### Most of

* Most of the students **are** ready.
* Most of the work **is** finished.

---

### All of

* All of the players **are** fit.
* All of the furniture **is** expensive.

---

### Any of

* Any of the boys **are** welcome. *(informal)*
* Any of the information **is** useful.

> ⚠️ In formal English, **"any of the boys is welcome"** is also acceptable when you mean **any single boy**. Placement exams usually avoid this ambiguity.


Don't look at **some**, **most**, **all**, or **any**.

Look only at the noun after **of**.

## One more thing

When I said **"depends on the noun,"** I meant **only** for this second family.

It does **not** apply to:

* One of
* Neither of
* Either of
* Each of

Those are **always singular**, no matter what plural noun comes after **of**.
