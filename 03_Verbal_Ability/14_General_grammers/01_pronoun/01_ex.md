Yes — this question is actually a **bit problematic**, because the explanation shown in your screenshot is oversimplified. Let's go through each option carefully.

The key topic here is **order of personal pronouns when two or more people are mentioned together**.

---

# 1. Option B — "She and I were busy with the project." ✅

> **She and I were busy with the project.**

This is grammatically correct.

Break it down:

* **She** → 3rd person
* **I** → 1st person

When referring to yourself together with another person, it is conventional to mention **the other person first and yourself second**.

So:

> **She and I** were busy. ✅

rather than:

> **I and she** were busy. ❌/very unnatural

### Compare:

> **Rahul and I** went to college. ✅

> **She and I** worked together. ✅

> **My brother and I** played cricket. ✅

This is the important rule the question is testing.

---

# 2. Option A — "He and You should go and buy some fruits." ❌

> **He and you should go and buy some fruits.**

The issue is **pronoun order**.

When **you** and another person are together, the usual order is:

> **You + the other person**

So:

> **You and he should go...** ✅

rather than:

> **He and you should go...** ❌/unnatural in formal exam grammar

### Why?

There is a traditional pronoun-order convention:

> **2nd person → 3rd person → 1st person**

Meaning:

> **You → he/she/they → I/we**

Examples:

> **You and he** should go. ✅

> **You and she** should go. ✅

> **You, he and I** should go. ✅

---

# 3. But there is something important here

The sentence:

> **He and you should go...**

is **not actually grammatically impossible in normal English**.

A native speaker can say it, and people will understand it.

The problem is that many **competitive-exam grammar questions** teach the conventional ordering:

> **You → he/she → I**

So the test expects:

> **You and he should go...**

The explanation in your screenshot says:

> "the speaker should place you before themselves"

That's not quite enough to explain A, because **the speaker isn't even mentioned as "I"** in A.

The real issue is the **conventional ordering of multiple pronouns**.

---

# 4. Option C — "You and I have misbehaved with the principal." ⚠️

This is where I think your question is **poorly designed**.

> **You and I have misbehaved with the principal.**

From the perspective of **pronoun order and case**, this is perfectly correct.

Why?

> You → 2nd person
> I → 1st person

And the usual order puts:

> **You before I**

So:

> **You and I** ✅

is completely normal.

For example:

> You and I are friends. ✅

> You and I should study. ✅

> You and I made the mistake. ✅

So **C should not be rejected simply because of pronoun order.**

There may be a separate issue with the wording **"misbehaved with the principal"**. More natural alternatives would be:

> You and I misbehaved **in front of the principal**.

or:

> You and I **behaved badly toward the principal**.

But the pronoun part itself is correct.

---

# 5. Option D — "He, I and you can build a house together." ❌

> **He, I and you can build a house together.**

The order is wrong according to the conventional exam rule.

It has:

> He → 3rd
> I → 1st
> you → 2nd

But the expected order is:

> **you → he → I**

So:

> **You, he and I can build a house together.** ✅

---

# 6. The Rule You Should Remember

For **multiple personal pronouns**, a common traditional grammar rule is:

### Normal/positive sentence:

> **2nd person → 3rd person → 1st person**

So:

> **You + he/she + I**

Example:

> **You, he and I** are responsible.

---

### Examples

> **You and I** should study. ✅

> **You and he** should go. ✅

> **She and I** worked together. ✅

> **You, she and I** attended the meeting. ✅

---

# 7. But There Is Another Rule for Fault/Blame

This is VERY important for exams.

When admitting **fault, guilt, or something negative**, traditionally the order changes:

> **1st person → 2nd person → 3rd person**

Why?

Because you put **yourself first when accepting blame**.

Example:

> **I, you and he** are responsible for the mistake.

Another:

> **I and my friends** were punished.

However, modern English doesn't always rigidly follow this rule, and natural usage often prefers other constructions.

---

# 8. Why Do We Say "You and I"?

This is another thing you should understand separately.

It's because **I** is the correct subject pronoun.

> You and **I** went there. ✅

Not:

> You and **me** went there. ❌

But when the pronouns are objects:

> The teacher called **you and me**. ✅

Not:

> The teacher called **you and I**. ❌

So:

### Subject → I

> **You and I** went there.

### Object → me

> He called **you and me**.
