Yes — **you are absolutely right about normal English usage.** The confusing part is the **exam's intended grammar distinction**.

The sentence is:

> **_____ parent came to meet the teacher.**

Options:

* Not
* None
* Neither
* No

### Why "No parent" sounds correct

You can absolutely say:

> **No parent came to meet the teacher.** ✅

This is grammatically correct English.

It means:

> **Not a single parent came to meet the teacher.**

So if the question asks for the **most suitable determiner**, **No** is actually a perfectly natural answer.

---

## Then why does the test say "Neither"?

Because **neither normally refers to two people/things**.

For example:

> **Neither parent came to meet the teacher.**

This means:

> **The father didn't come AND the mother didn't come.**

Here, "parent" is singular because **neither + singular countable noun** is standard:

> neither parent
> neither student
> neither answer
> neither option

So:

> **Neither parent came...** ✅

implies a specific pair of parents.

---

# "No" vs "Neither"

This is the important distinction:

| Sentence                 | Meaning                               |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------- |
| **No parent came.**      | Not a single parent came.             |
| **Neither parent came.** | Both of the two parents did not come. |

### Imagine a class meeting

Suppose there are **50 parents** who could potentially come.

> **No parent came to meet the teacher.** ✅

= 0 out of 50 came.

But suppose we're talking about **one student's two parents**:

> **Neither parent came to meet the teacher.** ✅

= Mother didn't come + Father didn't come.

---

# So what is happening in your question?

The question says:

> "Choose the most suitable **determiner**."

And the answer key apparently expects **Neither**.

That is likely because the test is assuming a **specific pair of parents** — for example, the student's mother and father.

But **without that context**, the sentence:

> **No parent came to meet the teacher.**

is completely natural and grammatically correct.

In fact, in ordinary English, if the intended meaning is simply **"not even one parent came"**, I would naturally choose:

> **No parent came to meet the teacher.**

---

## One more important thing: "None"

You might wonder:

> Why not **None parent**?

Because **none** normally doesn't directly work as a determiner before a singular noun.

❌ **None parent came.**

You can say:

> **None of the parents came.** ✅

So:

* **No parent** ✅
* **Neither parent** ✅
* **None parent** ❌
* **Not parent** ❌

---

### 🎯 For your exam, remember this

**NO + singular/plural noun**

> No parent came.
> No parents came.

**NEITHER + singular noun**

> Neither parent came.

**NONE OF + plural noun/pronoun**

> None of the parents came.

So your understanding is correct: **"No parent came to meet the teacher" is valid general English.** The test is choosing **"neither" because it is assuming two specific parents**, not because "no" is grammatically wrong.
