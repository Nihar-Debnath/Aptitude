This is another **very common placement exam rule**. It is about **Compound Adjectives**.

Many students get this wrong because they think:

> "There are two notes, so it should be ten-rupees."

But that's **not** how English works.

Let's understand it deeply.

---

# Question 1

### A1

> A Chinese gave me **two ten-rupees notes.** ❌

### B1

> A Chinese gave me **two ten-rupee notes.** ✅

---

# Step 1: Find the noun

What are we talking about?

```text
notes
```

The noun is **notes**.

---

# Step 2: What is "ten-rupee"?

Ask yourself:

Is **ten-rupee** the noun?

No.

It is **describing** the noun "notes."

It answers:

> **What kind of notes?**

→ Ten-rupee notes.

So **ten-rupee** is acting like an **adjective**.

---

# Very Important Rule

When a noun becomes part of an adjective, **it stays singular**.

## Formula

```text
Number + Singular Noun + Main Noun
```

Examples:

```text
a ten-rupee note
```

NOT

```text
a ten-rupees note ❌
```

---

Now make it plural.

```text
two ten-rupee notes
```

Notice:

Only **notes** becomes plural.

Not **rupee**.

---

# Why?

Because adjectives **never become plural** in English.

Look at these examples.

### Example 1

A red car

Two red cars

Did "red" become "reds"?

No.

---

### Example 2

A big house

Three big houses

Did "big" become "bigs"?

No.

---

Now

Ten-rupee is also acting as one adjective.

So

```text
ten-rupee
```

never changes.

---

# Another Example

One 100-page book

Two 100-page books

Not

❌ 100-pages books

---

# Question 2

### A2

> He stays in **five-stars hotels.** ❌

### B2

> He stays in **five-star hotels.** ✅

---

Again,

Find the noun.

```text
hotels
```

Now ask

What kind of hotels?

Answer

```text
five-star
```

This is an adjective.

Again,

Adjectives don't become plural.

So

```text
five-star hotel

five-star hotels
```

NOT

```text
five-stars hotels
```

---

# The General Rule

Whenever you see

```text
Number + Noun + Another Noun
```

The first noun usually stays **singular** if it's describing the second noun.

---

## Example 1

A five-year-old boy

Not

❌ five-years-old boy

---

Plural

Three five-year-old boys

Still

year

Not

years.

---

## Example 2

A two-hour meeting

Not

❌ two-hours meeting

Plural

Three two-hour meetings

Still

hour

---

## Example 3

A ten-kilogram bag

Not

❌ ten-kilograms bag

Plural

Two ten-kilogram bags

Still

kilogram

---

## Example 4

A six-foot wall

Not

❌ six-feet wall

Plural

Many six-foot walls

Still

foot

---

## Example 5

A five-day trip

Not

❌ five-days trip

Plural

Several five-day trips

Still

day

---

# Why Does This Happen?

Let's compare.

### Normal sentence

The trip lasted **five days**.

Here

Days

is the noun.

So it becomes plural.

---

### Compound adjective

It was a **five-day** trip.

Now

five-day

is one adjective.

Adjectives don't become plural.

---

# Compare Side by Side

| Normal Noun                       | Compound Adjective             |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| The trip lasted five **days**.    | It was a five-**day** trip.    |
| The bag weighs ten **kilograms**. | It is a ten-**kilogram** bag.  |
| The boy is five **years** old.    | He is a five-**year-old** boy. |
| The hotel has five **stars**.     | It is a five-**star** hotel.   |

This comparison is one of the easiest ways to remember the rule.

# Memory Trick

Whenever you see a **hyphen (-)** like:

* five-star
* ten-rupee
* two-hour
* three-day
* five-year-old

Immediately think:

> **This whole phrase is one adjective.**

And remember one golden rule:

> **Adjectives never become plural.**

So the pattern is always:

```text
Number + Singular Noun + Main Noun
```

Examples:

* a **ten-rupee** note
* a **five-star** hotel
* a **two-hour** meeting
* a **three-day** tour
* a **100-page** book
* a **five-year-old** child

---

## One small language note

The sentence in the image says:

> **A Chinese gave me two ten-rupee notes.**

Grammatically, many modern style guides prefer:

> **A Chinese person gave me two ten-rupee notes.**

because using **Chinese** alone as a noun for a person can sound dated or less natural in some contexts. However, the grammar rule being tested is **ten-rupee** vs. **ten-rupees**, and the correct answer remains **B1**.
