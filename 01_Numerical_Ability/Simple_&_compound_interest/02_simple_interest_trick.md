Yes! This is a **shortcut way of thinking about Simple Interest**, and it’s actually very useful for aptitude. Let me decode exactly what your teacher is doing.

### The normal SI formula

[
SI=\frac{P\times R\times T}{100}
]

Rearrange it:

[
SI=P\times\frac{RT}{100}
]

Now look at this:

[
\frac{RT}{100}
]

That is simply **RT%**.

So:

[
\boxed{SI=P\times(RT)%}
]

### What does that mean?

It means:

> **Simple Interest = (overall percentage of interest) × Principal**

where

[
\boxed{RT% = \text{overall interest percentage}}
]

---

### Example

Suppose:

* Principal = ₹20,000
* Rate = 8% per year
* Time = 3 years

Instead of immediately doing:

[
SI=\frac{20000\times8\times3}{100}
]

First calculate:

[
RT=8\times3=24
]

So the **overall interest = 24% of P**.

Now:

[
SI=24%\text{ of }20000
]

[
=\frac{24}{100}\times20000
]

[
\boxed{₹4,800}
]

That's the trick.

---

## Why is this useful?

Your teacher has basically converted the SI formula into:

> **Find the total percentage first → then find that percentage of P.**

So the method becomes:

### Step 1

Find:

[
\boxed{R\times T}
]

This gives the **overall interest percentage**.

### Step 2

Take that percentage of Principal:

[
\boxed{SI=(RT)%\text{ of }P}
]

### Step 3

If Amount is asked:

[
\boxed{A=P+SI}
]

---

### One more example

**₹15,000 is lent at 12% SI for 2.5 years. Find SI.**

First:

[
RT=12\times2.5=30
]

So overall interest = **30% of P**.

Therefore:

[
SI=30%\text{ of }15000
]

[
=\frac{30}{100}\times15000
]

[
\boxed{₹4,500}
]

So yes — **this is not a new formula**. It's just a **shortcut interpretation of the normal SI formula** that makes many questions faster.
