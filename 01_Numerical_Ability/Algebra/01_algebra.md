# There no algebra in written format



Think of algebra as:

> **“I don't know the number, so I call it (x), build a mathematical equation, and find (x).”**

---

# 1. What exactly is algebra?

Suppose I tell you:

> A number is increased by 5 and the result is 12. Find the number.

You don't know the number.

So call it:

\[
x
\]

"Number increased by 5" means:

\[
x+5
\]

"The result is 12" means:

\[
x+5=12
\]

Now solve:

\[
x=12-5
\]

\[
\boxed{x=7}
\]

That's algebra.


The important thing isn't the widget here; the important skill for aptitude is learning to **translate English → mathematical expression**.

---

# 2. The most important skill: translating words

You need to learn what common words mean mathematically.

| Words                     | Algebra           |
| ------------------------- | ----------------- |
| a number                  | \(x\)               |
| 5 more than a number      | \(x+5\)             |
| 5 less than a number      | \(x-5\)             |
| twice a number            | \(2x\)              |
| three times a number      | \(3x\)              |
| half of a number          | \(\frac{x}{2}\)     |
| one-third of a number     | \(\frac{x}{3}\)     |
| sum of two numbers        | \(x+y\)             |
| difference of two numbers | \(x-y\)             |
| product of two numbers    | \(xy\)              |
| 20% of a number           | \(\frac{20}{100}x\) |
| number increased by 20%   | \(1.2x\)            |
| number decreased by 20%   | \(0.8x\)            |

This table is **very important for aptitude**.

---

# 3. The basic algebra equation

Suppose:

> The sum of a number and 8 is 20. Find the number.

### Step 1: Unknown

\[
x
\]

### Step 2: Translate

"sum of a number and 8":

\[
x+8
\]

"equals 20":

\[
x+8=20
\]

### Step 3: Solve

We want (x) alone.

\[
x=20-8
\]

\[
\boxed{x=12}
\]

---

# 4. The golden rule for solving equations

Think of an equation as a **balance**.

For example:

\[
x+7=15
\]

To remove (+7), do the opposite operation:

\[
x=15-7
\]

### Opposite operations

| Given         | Do            |
| ------------- | ------------- |
| (x+5)         | subtract 5    |
| (x-5)         | add 5         |
| (5x)          | divide by 5   |
| (\frac{x}{5}) | multiply by 5 |

This one idea solves a HUGE number of basic algebra questions.

# 6. Example: "5 more than twice a number"

This is slightly more important.

> 5 more than twice a number is 25. Find the number.

Number:

\[
x
\]

Twice the number:

\[
2x
\]

5 more:

\[
2x+5
\]

Therefore:

\[
2x+5=25
\]

Subtract 5:

\[
2x=20
\]

Divide by 2:

\[
\boxed{x=10}
\]

# 7. Algebra questions involving ages

These are **very common in aptitude**.

Example:

> The age of a father is 3 times the age of his son. Their total age is 48 years. Find their ages.

Don't randomly guess.

Let son's age:

\[
x
\]

Father's age:

\[
3x
\]

Total:

\[
x+3x=48
\]

\[
4x=48
\]

\[
x=12
\]

Therefore:

Son:

\[
\boxed{12}
\]

Father:

\[
\boxed{36}
\]

---

# 8. Example: consecutive numbers

Another common algebra pattern.

> The sum of two consecutive numbers is 35. Find them.

What does **consecutive** mean?

Numbers immediately next to each other:

\[
5,6
\]

or

\[
10,11
\]

or

\[
20,21
\]

So let first number:

\[
x
\]

Next number:

\[
x+1
\]

Their sum:

\[
x+(x+1)=35
\]

\[
2x+1=35
\]

\[
2x=34
\]

\[
x=17
\]

So numbers are:

\[
\boxed{17,18}
\]

# 9. Three consecutive numbers

Suppose:

> The sum of three consecutive numbers is 72. Find them.

Let first:

\[
x
\]

Second:

\[
x+1
\]

Third:

\[
x+2
\]

Therefore:

\[
x+(x+1)+(x+2)=72
\]

\[
3x+3=72
\]

\[
3x=69
\]

\[
x=23
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{23,24,25}
\]

# 10. Algebra + percentage

This is where algebra becomes REALLY useful.

Suppose:

> A number is increased by 20% and becomes 240. Find the original number.

Let original number:

\[
x
\]

20% increase:

\[
x+\frac{20}{100}x
\]

\[
=1.2x
\]

Therefore:

\[
1.2x=240
\]

\[
x=\frac{240}{1.2}
\]

\[
\boxed{200}
\]

So algebra connects directly with the **Percentage** topic you already studied.

---

# 11. Algebra + ratio

Suppose:

> The ratio of A and B is 2:3 and their sum is 50. Find A and B.

Ratio:

\[
A:B=2:3
\]

The total number of parts:

\[
2+3=5
\]

One part:

\[
\frac{50}{5}=10
\]

Therefore:

\[
A=2\times10=20
\]

\[
B=3\times10=30
\]

This is technically ratio rather than pure algebra, but the same idea is being used: **represent unknown quantities mathematically.**

---

# 12. Algebra + simple equations

You'll also see questions like:

> If (5x-7=28), find (x).

Just reverse the operations.

\[
5x-7=28
\]

Add 7:

\[
5x=35
\]

Divide by 5:

\[
\boxed{x=7}
\]

---

# 13. What types of algebra should YOU study?

For a placement aptitude exam like TCS, I'd break Algebra into these small chunks:

### 🟢 Level 1 — Basic equations

* (x+5=20)
* (3x=27)
* (2x+5=25)
* equations with fractions

### 🟢 Level 2 — Word problems

* numbers
* ages
* consecutive numbers
* sum/difference
* products

### 🟢 Level 3 — Ratio-based algebra

* two numbers in ratio
* three numbers in ratio
* ages in ratio

### 🟢 Level 4 — Percentage-based algebra

* increase/decrease
* original value
* successive changes

### 🟡 Level 5 — Quadratic equations

Things like:

\[
x^2-5x+6=0
\]

These are a little different because the unknown appears as (x^2).

### 🟡 Level 6 — Algebraic identities

The important ones:

\[
(a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2
\]

\[
(a-b)^2=a^2-2ab+b^2
\]

\[
a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b)
\]

These can give you **very fast shortcuts** in aptitude.

# 🧠 The biggest mindset change

Don't think:

> "Oh no, algebra. I need to remember complicated formulas."

Instead think:

> **Unknown → call it (x) → translate the sentence → make equation → solve.**

For example:

**Question:**

> Five more than twice a number is 31.

Your brain should immediately go:

**number → (x)**

**twice → (2x)**

**five more → (2x+5)**

**is 31 → (=31)**

So:

\[
2x+5=31
\]

\[
2x=26
\]

\[
\boxed{x=13}
\]

That's algebra. ❤️

And honestly, **this is a topic where we should NOT jump straight into hard questions**. Since you're building this for your aptitude exam, the smart route is to learn it in small chunks: **basic equations → word translation → ages/numbers → ratios → percentages → quadratic equations → shortcuts**, with 3–5 questions after each chunk.
