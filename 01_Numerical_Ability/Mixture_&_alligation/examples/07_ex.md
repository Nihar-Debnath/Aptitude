Yes! This one is a **mixture/alligation-style profit question**, and there is a really nice shortcut. Let's understand the logic first.

We have:

* Total sugar = **1000 kg**
* Some is sold at **8% profit**
* Rest is sold at **18% profit**
* Overall profit = **14%**
* Find quantity sold at **18% profit**

### Step 1: Think of the profit percentages as the "mixture"

We are combining:

[
8% \quad\text{and}\quad 18%
]

to get an overall:

[
14%
]

So treat them exactly like the concentration problem we just did.

```text
8% ---------------- 14% ---------------- 18%
          6%                  4%
```

Now use the **opposite distances**:

[
\text{8% quantity : 18% quantity}
=================================

(18-14):(14-8)
]

[
=4:6
]

[
=\boxed{2:3}
]

So the quantities are:

[
\boxed{2:3}
]

meaning:

* 2 parts sold at 8%
* 3 parts sold at 18%

---

### Step 2: Total quantity = 1000 kg

Total parts:

[
2+3=5
]

Therefore one part:

[
1000\div5=200
]

18% profit quantity = 3 parts:

[
3\times200
]

[
\boxed{600\text{ kg}}
]

# ✅ Answer: 600 kg

---

### 🧠 The important connection to your previous question

This is **exactly the same Alligation concept**.

Previously:

[
5% + 15% \rightarrow 10%
]

Here:

[
8% + 18% \rightarrow 14%
]

Just replace **concentration** with **profit percentage**.

The moment you see:

> One part at X%, another part at Y%, and overall result is Z%

you should immediately think:

[
\boxed{\text{Alligation}}
]

And remember:

[
\boxed{\text{Quantity ratio}=
(\text{higher}-\text{mean}):(\text{mean}-\text{lower})}
]

So here:

[
18-14:14-8
]

[
4:6=2:3
]

Then use the total 1000 kg. 🔥
