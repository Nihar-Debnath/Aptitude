Yes! This is the **core idea behind the Alligation method**, and once you understand *why* the cheaper/dearer concept works, the formula becomes much less scary.

Let's build it from intuition rather than memorizing the cross.

---

# 🧪 Cheaper & Dearer — What does it mean?

Suppose we have two things:

* **Cheaper item** → ₹20/kg
* **Dearer item** → ₹40/kg

We mix them together.

Obviously, the final mixture's price must be **somewhere between ₹20 and ₹40**.

For example:

\[
₹20 \quad+\quad ₹40 \quad\rightarrow\quad ₹30
\]

The question is:

> **How much of ₹20 material and how much of ₹40 material should we take to get ₹30?**

---

## Think about the average

If I take:

* 1 kg of ₹20 material
* 1 kg of ₹40 material

Total cost:

\[
20+40=₹60
\]

Total quantity:

\[
2kg
\]

Average:

\[
60/2=₹30
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{₹20:₹40=1:1}
\]

Makes sense because ₹30 is exactly halfway between ₹20 and ₹40.

---

# Now let's change the required mixture

Suppose we want the mixture to cost **₹25/kg**.

We have:

\[
₹20 \qquad ₹40
\]

but want:

\[
₹25
\]

Notice something important:

₹25 is **very close to ₹20**.

Therefore, we need **more of the cheaper ₹20 material** and less of the ₹40 material.

Let's find exactly how much.

---

## The "distance" idea 🔥

Look at how far ₹25 is from each price:

From cheaper:

\[
25-20=5
\]

From dearer:

\[
40-25=15
\]

So the mixture is:

* **5 away from cheaper**
* **15 away from dearer**

And here's the beautiful part:

### The quantities work in the opposite ratio.

\[
\boxed{\text{Cheaper quantity : Dearer quantity}
=

15:5}
\]

\[
=3:1
\]

So:

\[
\boxed{3kg\text{ cheaper}+1kg\text{ dearer}}
\]

Let's verify it:

Cost of 3 kg cheaper:

\[
3\times20=60
\]

Cost of 1 kg dearer:

\[
1\times40=40
\]

Total:

\[
100
\]

Total quantity:

\[
4kg
\]

Average:

\[
100/4=\boxed{₹25}
\]

Perfect!

---

# 🤯 But WHY is the ratio opposite?

This is the part I really want you to understand.

Imagine you're trying to bring the **₹40** price down to **₹25**.

₹40 is **15 above** ₹25.

That's a big difference.

To pull ₹40 down to ₹25, you need a **large amount of the ₹20 material**.

The cheaper material pulls the average downward.

So:

\[
\boxed{\text{Farther away → MORE quantity of the opposite item}}
\]

That's why the numbers cross.

---

# The Alligation Diagram

For:

* Cheaper = ₹20
* Dearer = ₹40
* Mean = ₹25

We write:

```text
       CHEAPER              DEARER
         ₹20                  ₹40
           \                  /
            \                /
             \              /
                ₹25
              MIXTURE
```

Now calculate the distances:

```text
       ₹20                  ₹40
         \                  /
          \                /
           \              /
            ₹25
```

Distance from ₹40 to ₹25:

\[
40-25=15
\]

Distance from ₹25 to ₹20:

\[
25-20=5
\]

Cross them:

```text
       ₹20                  ₹40
         \                  /
          15              5
            \            /
               ₹25
```

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\text{Cheaper : Dearer}=15:5=3:1}
\]

---

# ⭐ The Golden Rule

If:

\[
C=\text{Cheaper}
\]

\[
D=\text{Dearer}
\]

\[
M=\text{Mean/Mixture price}
\]

Then:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Cheaper quantity : Dearer quantity}
=

(D-M):(M-C)
}
\]

Notice:

### For cheaper quantity, use the distance from **mixture to dearer**.

### For dearer quantity, use the distance from **cheaper to mixture**.

So:

\[
\boxed{C:D=(D-M):(M-C)}
\]

# Let's do another one

Tea A costs ₹60/kg.

Tea B costs ₹100/kg.

We want a mixture costing ₹70/kg.

Ask yourself first:

> ₹70 is much closer to ₹60 or ₹100?

It's closer to **₹60**.

Therefore, we should need **more ₹60 tea**.

Now alligation:

\[
60\qquad100
\]

Mixture:

\[
70
\]

Differences:

\[
100-70=30
\]

\[
70-60=10
\]

Therefore:

\[
\text{Cheaper : Dearer}=30:10
\]

\[
\boxed{3:1}
\]

So we take:

\[
\boxed{3kg\text{ of ₹60 tea}+1kg\text{ of ₹100 tea}}
\]

Check:

\[
3(60)+1(100)=280
\]

Total quantity:

\[
4kg
\]

Average:

\[
280/4=\boxed{₹70}
\]

# 🧠 The intuition you should carry into the exam

Don't initially think:

> "Which numbers do I cross?"

Instead think:

### 1. Identify cheaper and dearer

```text
CHEAPER ←--------- MIXTURE ---------> DEARER
```

### 2. See where the mixture is closer.

If mixture is closer to **cheaper**:

➡️ More cheaper material
➡️ Less dearer material

If mixture is closer to **dearer**:

➡️ More dearer material
➡️ Less cheaper material

### 3. Use opposite distances

\[
\boxed{
\text{Quantity of one} \propto
\text{distance of the other from mixture}
}
\]

That's the real logic behind the **Alligation rule**.

Once this clicks, the famous cross-method stops looking like some random aptitude trick. 😄
