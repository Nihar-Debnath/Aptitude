# Alligation Rule

Suppose you have two things:

* **Lower value** = (L)
* **Higher value** = (H)
* Desired mixture value = (M)

The arrangement is:

```text
             HIGHER (H)
                  \
                   \
                    MIDDLE (M)
                   /
                  /
              LOWER (L)
```

Now calculate:

[
H-M
]

and

[
M-L
]

### The important rule:

[
\boxed{
\text{Quantity of LOWER}:\text{Quantity of HIGHER}
==================================================

(H-M):(M-L)
}
]

**Notice the order carefully:**

```text
LOWER quantity  → HIGHER - MIDDLE

HIGHER quantity → MIDDLE - LOWER
```

That's the actual rule.

---

# 🔥 The easiest formula to memorize

If you have:

```text
LOWER       MIXTURE       HIGHER
  L            M             H
```

then:

[
\boxed{
L:H=(H-M):(M-L)
}
]

That's it.

### Think of it like this:

```text
LOWER                    HIGHER
  L                         H
   \                       /
    \                     /
     \                   /
          MIXTURE M
```

The **higher value minus mixture** goes to the **lower quantity**.

The **mixture minus lower value** goes to the **higher quantity**.

## One final example

Rice:

* ₹5/kg
* ₹8/kg
* mixture = ₹6/kg

Then:

[
\text{₹5 rice : ₹8 rice}
========================

(8-6):(6-5)
]

[
=2:1
]

So:

**₹5 rice = 2 parts**

**₹8 rice = 1 part**

Why?

Because ₹6 is much closer to ₹5 than ₹8, so we need **more ₹5 rice** and less ₹8 rice.

### Why does Alligation do this?

Look at the distances:

```text
8% -------- 14% ---- 18%
      6%          4%
```

The **8%** is 6 points away from 14%.

The **18%** is only 4 points away.

Therefore, we need **more 18%** to balance the larger gap of the 8%.

That's why:

[
\boxed{8%:18%=4:6=2:3}
]

**The 6 goes to the 18% quantity, and the 4 goes to the 8% quantity.**

So your proposed **18% = 2, 8% = 3** would actually produce **12%**, not 14%.
