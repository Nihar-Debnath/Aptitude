Yep! This is a **combined average** question. The easiest way is to think in terms of **total salary**.

### Given:

* Average salary of **all employees** = ₹8,000
* Average salary of **7 technicians** = ₹12,000
* Average salary of **remaining employees** = ₹6,000
* Total employees = ?

Let's call the number of remaining employees **(x)**.

So total employees:

\[
7+x
\]

---

### Step 1: Find total salary of technicians

There are 7 technicians and their average is ₹12,000.

Remember:

\[
\boxed{\text{Total}=\text{Average}\times\text{Number}}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\text{Technicians' total salary}
=12000\times7
=84000
\]

---

### Step 2: Find total salary of the remaining employees

There are (x) remaining employees.

Their average is ₹6,000.

So:

\[
\text{Remaining salary}=6000x
\]

---

### Step 3: Find total salary of ALL employees

There are (7+x) employees.

Their average is ₹8,000.

Therefore:

\[
\text{Total salary}=8000(7+x)
\]

---

### Step 4: Equate the two ways of calculating total salary

The total salary of everyone is:

\[
\text{Technicians' salary}+\text{Remaining salary}
\]

So:

\[
84000+6000x=8000(7+x)
\]

Expand:

\[
84000+6000x=56000+8000x
\]

Move things around:

\[
84000-56000=8000x-6000x
\]

\[
28000=2000x
\]

\[
x=14
\]

So there are **14 remaining employees**.

But the question asks for **total employees**:

\[
7+14=\boxed{21}
\]

### ✅ Answer: **A. 21**

## 🔥 There's an even faster trick for this type

Look at the three averages:

**Technicians:** ₹12,000
**Everyone:** ₹8,000
**Remaining:** ₹6,000

Think of the overall average ₹8,000 as the **balance point**.

Technicians are:

\[
12000-8000=4000
\]

**₹4,000 above** the overall average.

Remaining employees are:

\[
8000-6000=2000
\]

**₹2,000 below** the overall average.

For the average to remain ₹8,000, the "extra" from technicians must equal the "deficit" from the remaining employees:

\[
7\times4000=x\times2000
\]

\[
28000=2000x
\]

\[
x=14
\]

Then:

\[
7+14=\boxed{21}
\]

This is called the **alligation/weighted-average shortcut**, and it's VERY useful in aptitude exams.



























No problem. Forget the formula for a moment. **Let's understand why the shortcut works.**

We have:

* Technicians' average = **₹12,000**
* Overall average = **₹8,000**
* Remaining employees' average = **₹6,000**

The key question is:

> **Why does the overall average become ₹8,000?**

---

### Step 1: Imagine ₹8,000 is the "normal" salary

If **everyone** earned exactly ₹8,000, the overall average would obviously be ₹8,000.

Now look at the technicians.

Each technician earns an average of ₹12,000.

That's:

\[
12000-8000=\boxed{4000}
\]

So each technician has **₹4,000 extra** compared to the overall average.

There are 7 technicians:

\[
7\times4000=\boxed{28000}
\]

So technicians have a total of **₹28,000 extra**.

---

### Step 2: Look at the remaining employees

Their average is only ₹6,000.

Compared to the overall ₹8,000:

\[
8000-6000=\boxed{2000}
\]

So each remaining employee has **₹2,000 less** than the overall average.

---

### Step 3: The extra and shortage must balance

This is the whole trick.

Technicians have:

**₹28,000 extra**

The remaining employees must therefore have:

**₹28,000 shortage**

Each remaining employee has a ₹2,000 shortage.

So:

\[
\frac{28000}{2000}=14
\]

Therefore there are **14 remaining employees**.

And technicians = 7.

So total:

\[
7+14=\boxed{21}
\]

---

## 🧠 Think of it like a balance

Imagine ₹8,000 is the middle:

```text
Technicians              Overall              Remaining
  ₹12,000                 ₹8,000                ₹6,000
     ↑                       |                     ↓
   +₹4,000                   |                   -₹2,000
```

For the overall average to stay at ₹8,000:

```text
7 technicians × ₹4,000 extra
        =
14 employees × ₹2,000 shortage
```

\[
7\times4000=14\times2000
\]

That's it.

### The shortcut is basically:

> **Extra above average = Shortage below average**

And then:

\[
\boxed{\text{Number on one side}\times\text{Extra}
=

\text{Number on other side}\times\text{Shortage}}
\]

Don't worry about memorizing it yet. **Understand this balance idea first.**

## Here's the part I REALLY want you to understand

Forget salaries. Imagine **money in a balance**.

We want everyone to average **₹8,000**.

```text
Technician:
₹12,000
   ↓
₹8,000 target
   ↑
₹4,000 EXTRA
```

Every technician gives **₹4,000 extra**.

7 technicians give:

\[
7\times4000=₹28,000\text{ extra}
\]

Now:

```text
Remaining employee:
₹6,000
   ↓
₹8,000 target
   ↑
₹2,000 SHORT
```

Every remaining employee is ₹2,000 short.

So how many ₹2,000 shortages do we need to cancel **₹28,000 extra**?

\[
₹28,000
\div
₹2,000
=14
\]

That's why there are **14 remaining employees**.

---

### One sentence to remember

> **The high-salary group creates an EXTRA, and the low-salary group must create an equal SHORTAGE.**

That's the entire shortcut.
