This is a **very useful property of combinations**:

\[
\boxed{{}^nC_r={}^nC_{n-r}}
\]

It's called the **symmetry property of combinations**.

### What does it mean?

It says:

> Choosing **r items out of n** is the same as choosing the **items you leave behind**.

For example:

\[
{}^ {10}C_3 = {}^{10}C_{10-3}
\]

\[
\boxed{{}^{10}C_3={}^{10}C_7}
\]

Both have the same answer:

\[
{}^{10}C_3=120
\]

and

\[
{}^{10}C_7=120
\]

---

### Why?

Suppose there are **10 students** and you need to select **3**.

You can think of it in two ways:

**Method 1:** Select the 3 students who will come.

**Method 2:** Select the 7 students who will NOT come.

Once you've selected the 7 who stay out, the remaining 3 are automatically selected.

So:

\[
\boxed{\text{Choose 3}=\text{Leave 7}}
\]

That's why:

\[
{}^{10}C_3={} ^{10}C_7
\]

---

### 🔥 Why is this useful in exams?

It makes calculations much faster.

Suppose:

\[
{}^{15}C_{13}
\]

Calculating (13) terms would be annoying.

Use the property:

\[
{}^{15}C_{13}={}^{15}C_{15-13}
\]

\[
={}^{15}C_2
\]

Now use your previous shortcut:

\[
{}^{15}C_2
=

\frac{15\times14}{1\times2}
\]

\[
=\boxed{105}
\]

### So combine your two tricks:

**Trick 1:**

\[
\boxed{{}^nC_r={}^nC_{n-r}}
\]

Make (r) smaller.

**Trick 2:**

Then use:

\[
{}^nC_r=
\frac{n(n-1)(n-2)\cdots}{1\times2\times3\cdots r}
\]

So for:

\[
{}^{20}C_{17}
\]

First:

\[
{}^{20}C_{17}={} ^{20}C_3
\]

Then:

\[
\frac{20\times19\times18}{1\times2\times3}
\]

Much faster. 🚀
