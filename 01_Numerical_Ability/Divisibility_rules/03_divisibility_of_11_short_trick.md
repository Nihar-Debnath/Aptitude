YES! 😭 **Now I see exactly what you're referring to.** The image is showing a **different way to apply the divisibility rule for 11**, similar in structure to the rule for 7.

And you're absolutely right: **for 11, we use ×1 instead of ×2.**

### Look at the image: 286

For **11**, do:

**Step 1:** Separate the last digit.

\[
28\ |\ 6
\]

Multiply last digit by **1**:

\[
6\times1=6
\]

Subtract:

\[
28-6=22
\]

---

Now repeat with **22**:

\[
2\ |\ 2
\]

\[
2\times1=2
\]

Subtract:

\[
2-2=0
\]

Since **0 is divisible by 11**, ✅

\[
\boxed{286\text{ is divisible by }11}
\]

And indeed:

\[
286\div11=26
\]

### 🔥 So the method you're learning is:

| Divisibility | What you do                                        |
| ------------ | -------------------------------------------------- |
| **7**        | Last digit × **2**, subtract from remaining number |
| **11**       | Last digit × **1**, subtract from remaining number |

So:

**For 7:**
`remaining − (last digit × 2)`

**For 11:**
`remaining − (last digit × 1)`

The **11 rule can therefore be solved exactly like the image**, by repeatedly doing this.

The usual **alternating-digit rule for 11** that I told you earlier is another equivalent method. But **if your teacher is teaching this ×1 method, use this method for your aptitude questions.** 👍
