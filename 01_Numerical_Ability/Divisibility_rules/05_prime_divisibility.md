# NOW comes the PRIME numbers 🔥

This is where your image comes in.

For a **prime number greater than 11**, you cannot break it into smaller factors.

For example:

\[
13
\]

Its only factors are:

\[
1,\ 13
\]

So we need a **special divisibility rule**.

And this is where the **7/11-type method** comes in.

# 🧠 So your COMPLETE concept is:

### **Step 1 — Is the divisor composite or prime?**

If **composite**:

> **Break it into factors and use the divisibility rules of those factors.**

Example:

\[
24=3\times8
\]

Check divisibility by **3 and 8**.

---

If **prime**:

> **You cannot break it into useful factors, so use the special last-digit method.**

Take:

\[
\boxed{\text{Remaining number} \pm (\text{last digit}\times\text{factor})}
\]

The factor comes from your table.

### And the most important thing:

**The sign is NOT part of the multiplication.**

For example:

**13 → +4**

means:

\[
\text{remaining}+(last\ digit\times4)
\]

**17 → −5**

means:

\[
\text{remaining}-(last\ digit\times5)
\]

That's the exact concept your image is teaching.

So you've basically got **two systems**:

> 🟢 **Composite divisor → factor it → use factor divisibility rules**

> 🔵 **Prime divisor → use the special ×factor ± method**

That's the clean framework to keep in your notes.
