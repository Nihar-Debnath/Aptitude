Yes! This one has a **very specific HCF–LCM trick**. Once you understand the wording, it's actually very easy. 😄

### First, understand the question

> HCF of two numbers is **23** and the **other two factors of their LCM** are **13 and 14**.

The important idea is:

If two numbers have HCF = 23, we can write them as:

[
23\times(\text{something})
]

So let the two numbers be:

[
23x \quad\text{and}\quad 23y
]

The question tells us that the **other two factors** are 13 and 14.

So:

[
x=13,\qquad y=14
]

Therefore the two numbers are:

[
23\times13=299
]

and

[
23\times14=322
]

So the larger number is:

[
\boxed{322}
]

---

## 🧠 But what does "other two factors" mean?

This is the part that is probably confusing you.

Suppose we have two numbers:

[
23\times13
]

and

[
23\times14
]

Their LCM contains:

* the common HCF → **23**
* the remaining factors → **13 and 14**

So we can think of it as:

```text
Number 1 = 23 × 13
Number 2 = 23 × 14
            ↑     ↑
          extra  extra
```

The question gives you:

```text
HCF = 23
Other factors = 13 and 14
```

Therefore:

```text
First number  = 23 × 13 = 299
Second number = 23 × 14 = 322
```

### ⭐ The shortcut

Whenever you see:

> **HCF = H, and the other factors are a and b**

Immediately think:

[
\boxed{\text{Numbers}=H\times a,\ H\times b}
]

Here:

[
23\times13,\quad23\times14
]

So:

[
\boxed{322}
]

### One small terminology point

I think by **"iasliong"** you mean the **logic/reasoning** behind the question. The logic is simply:

> **HCF is the common part of both numbers. The "other factors" are the parts left after removing that common HCF.**

So:

[
\boxed{23\times13\quad\text{and}\quad23\times14}
]

That's the whole idea.
